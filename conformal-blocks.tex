\subsection{References}

In the main text I include a few references, for things I think need 
more explanation than I can provide; I didn't try to give correct 
attributions of the origin of the ideas
and constructions we discuss, mostly because I am not capable.
But let me add a few historical references here.

The idea of conformal block grows out of the structure of 2-dimensional conformal field
theory, which I believe was first clearly elucidated in \cite{BPZ1984}.
Vertex algebras are an abstraction of the structure of operator products
in these theories, and in this form were originally defined in \cite{Borcherds1986}.
The affine and Virasoro vertex algebras, which are basic and important examples for our story,
were defined in \cite{FrenkelKac1980,FrenkelZhu1992}.
The structure of modular tensor category arising from conformal field theory 
was first described (implicitly) in \cite{MooreSeiberg1989ClassicalQuantum},
and formalized in categorical language in \cite{Turaev1992ModularCategories}.

\section{Basic data}

\subsection{Vertex algebras and modules}

The first basic datum of the course is a 
\ti{vertex algebra} $\vertex$. 
A standard reference is
\cite{kac1998vertex}; our philosophy is close in spirit to
\cite{FrenkelBenZvi2004VertexAlgebrasCurves}.

Recall that an algebra is a vector space $\cA$ with some additional
structure: a linear map $\cA \to \End \cA$, obeying some conditions.

Similarly a vertex algebra is a vector space $\vertex$ with additional structure.
We think of $a \in \vertex$ as objects which can be inserted at a point in the complex plane, giving an object $a(z)$ which varies analytically with $z$. But moreover we can insert multiple objects
at multiple points of the plane.
When we insert $a(z)$ and $b(0)$, and bring $z$ close to 
$0$, we think of the composite as being another 
element of $\vertex$ (or more precisely some completion of
$\vertex$, associated to a small disc around $0$).

Then for any $a \in \vertex$, 
$a(z)$ should provide an endomorphism $Y(a,z) \in \End \vertex$,
depending analytically on $z$.
We don't expect that $Y(a,z)$ exists at $z = 0$; our motivation is
from local defects in quantum field theory, and often there are
singularities when two defects are placed at the same point.
But still we should be able to expand in a series around $z = 0$.
So, for $b \in \vertex$ we will have an expansion
\begin{equation} \label{eq:y-series}
  Y(a,z) b = \sum_{n \in \bbZ} z^{-n-1} a_{(n)} b
\end{equation}
where each $a_{(n)} \in \End \vertex$. We require
that this expansion is meromorphic as opposed to an essential
singularity: i.e. $a_{(n)} b = 0$ for $n \gg 0$.
% One useful special case is that $\vertex$ is a module over itself
% (``vacuum module''):
% in that case we just write $Y(a,z)$ for $Y^\vertex(a,z)$,
% and $a_{(n)}$ for $a^\vertex_{(n)}$.

% Usually we consider $\vertex$-modules for which 
% \eqref{eq:y-series} is a Laurent series,
% i.e. $a^\vrep_{(n)} \chi = 0$ for $n \gg 0$, i.e. $Y^\vrep(a,z) \chi$ is formally meromorphic in $z$. In particular, we require
% that $\vertex$ itself has this property.

When we have $a, b, c \in \vertex$ we also can consider
the combined object $a(z) b(w) c(0)$. This 
object should again lie in $\vertex$, depending
analytically on $z$ and $w$ away from $z = 0$ or $w = 0$, 
except that it can also have a pole at $z = w$.
This means that it may have different Laurent expansions
around $z = w = 0$ depending on whether
$\abs{w} < \abs{z}$ or $\abs{w} > \abs{z}$.\footnote{Recall the example
\begin{equation}
 f(z,w) = \frac{1}{z-w} = \begin{cases} z^{-1} \sum_{n \ge 0} (w/z)^n & \text{ for } \abs{w} < \abs{z}, \\ -w^{-1} \sum_{n \ge 0} (z/w)^n & \text{ for } \abs{w} > \abs{z}. \end{cases}
\end{equation}
These two Laurent expansions are different but related.
Both of them give $1$ if you multiply them by $z-w$.
Thus their difference, in $\bbC[[z^{\pm 1}, w^{\pm 1}]]$, 
is annihilated by multiplication by $z-w$.
This is an algebraic version of saying that they are
Laurent expansions of the same function.
}
We interpret these two expansions as $Y(a,z) Y(b,w) c$ and $Y(b,w) Y(a,z) c$ respectively.\footnote{
The idea is that the operator closer to $0$ acts first,
and then the operator farther from $0$ acts on the 
resulting object. We won't attempt to formalize this statement
precisely now; for physicists it is an instance of \ti{radial quantization}; it can be formalized in the language of \ti{holomorphic factorization algebras}, see e.g. \cite{CostelloGwilliam2021FA2}.}

Now we can make the definition:

\begin{defn}[Vertex algebra] \label{def:vertex-algebra}

A \emph{vertex algebra} is a quadruple \((\vertex, \vac, T, Y)\) where
\(\vertex\) is a vector space, \(\vac \in \vertex \),
\(T \in \mathrm{End}(\vertex)\),
and
\begin{equation}
Y(\,\cdot\,,z): \vertex \to \mathrm{End}(\vertex)[[z,z^{-1}]],\qquad
Y(a,z) = \sum_{n\in \mathbb{Z}} a_{(n)} z^{-n-1},
\end{equation}
is a linear map, such that:

\begin{enumerate}

\item  
$Y(\vac,z) = \bid_{\vertex}$ \, ,

\item
$Y(a,z) \vac \in \vertex[[z]]$ and $Y(a,0) \vac = a$ (i.e. $a_{(n)} \vac = 0$ for $n \ge 0$ and $a_{(-1)} \vac = a$) ,

\item
$[T,\,Y(a,z)] = \partial_z\, Y(a,z)$ and $T \vac = 0$ ,

\item For all $a,b,c \in \vertex$
there exists $F(z,w) \in \vertex[[z,w]][z^{-1},w^{-1},(z-w)^{-1}]$, such that
$Y(a,z) Y(b,w) c$ and 
$Y(b,w) Y(a,z) c$ are the
formal Laurent expansion of $F(z,w)$ in the regions
$\abs{w} < \abs{z}$ and $\abs{z} < \abs{w}$
respectively.
Or, equivalently: there exists $N$ such that $(z-w)^N Y(a,z) Y(b,w) c = (z-w)^N Y(b,w) Y(a,z)  c$.
\end{enumerate}

Moreover we have a grading
$\vertex = \bigoplus_{n \in \bbN} \vertex_n$, where
each $\vertex_n$ is finite-dimensional, $\vertex_0 = \bbC \vac$,
$T$ has degree $1$, and $a_{(n)}$ has degree $(\deg a)-n-1$.
\end{defn}

We will also sometimes use the notation $a_h = a_{((\deg a)+h-1)}$;
then $a_h$ has degree $-h$.

Some remarks:
\begin{itemize}
\item Some authors would allow more general notions of
vertex algebra, and would call ours \ti{CFT-type} vertex algebras;
but this is broad enough for our purposes;
our goal is mostly to explore the nicest examples.

\item Property (4) is sometimes called the ``locality'' axiom: it 
expresses the idea that singularities in $a(z) b(w)$ occur only
at $z=w$, not anywhere else. We also say: $Y(a,z)$ and $Y(b,w)$ are \ti{local} with respect to one another.

\item Note that the grading implies that for each \(a, b \in \vertex \) we have \(a_{(n)}b=0\) for all \(n\gg 0\), i.e.

Here are two key facts. Proper proofs can be found e.g.
in \cite{FrenkelBenZvi2004VertexAlgebrasCurves} chapter 3.

\begin{thm}[Associativity of vertex algebras] \label{thm:vertex-algebra-associativity} For any $a,b \in \vertex$,
\begin{equation}
Y(a,z) Y(b,w) = Y(Y(a,z-w)b,w) = \sum_{n \in \bbZ} (z-w)^{-n-1} Y(a_{(n)} b, w) \, \in \End \vertex[[z^{\pm 1},w^{\pm 1}]] ,  
\end{equation}
where in the middle and
on the right we mean the expansion in the region $\abs{z} > \abs{w}$.
\end{thm}

\autoref{thm:vertex-algebra-associativity} expresses the idea that
``$a(z) b(w) c(0)$'' should stand for some 
well-defined analytic object
which we can expand either in the region $\abs{z} > \abs{w} > 0$
or in the region $\abs{w} > \abs{z-w} > 0$.

Here is a proof sketch. 
First you show using (1)-(3) that
\begin{equation}
  Y(a,z) Y(b,w) \vac = Y(Y(a,z-w) b, w) \vac \, .
\end{equation}
(You use the integrated form of (3), namely 
$\e^{z T} Y(a,w) \e^{-z T} = Y(a,z+w)$, and combining
that with (2), $\e^{z T} b = Y(b,z) \vac$.)
You want to derive the same thing with $\vac$ replaced
by any other vector, i.e. you want to insert a third
object at $0$. So act on both sides with $Y(c, u)$ on the left: this amounts to inserting that third object at large $\abs{u}$.
You get
\begin{equation}
  Y(c,u) Y(a,z) Y(b,w) \vac = Y(c,u) Y(Y(a,z-w) b, w) \vac \, .
\end{equation}
Then you want to move the $Y(c,u)$ to the right
and substitute $u=0$. 
You expect this should work, because of analytic 
continuation: the identity is true at large $\abs{u}$, and $u$ 
can avoid $z$ and $w$ on its path to $u=0$, 
so there should be no problem.
The way you realize this idea using \autoref{def:vertex-algebra}
is that you multiply both sides by
a big power $(u-w)^N (u-z)^N$, then the commutation is 
allowed using axiom (4), and then set $u = 0$ and divide
out the harmless $w^N z^N$.

\begin{align}

\end{align}

\subsection{Universal enveloping vertex algebras}

Many of the vertex algebras we consider are of a simple sort:
they are \ti{universal enveloping vertex algebras}.

Recall the universal enveloping algebra of a Lie algebra:
given a Lie algebra $\fg$, $U(\fg)$ is an 
associative algebra which is ``freely generated by $\fg$,''
in the sense that there is a map $\fg \to U(\fg)$ of Lie algebras, 
with a universal property: 
for any associative algebra $A$, any map $\fg \to A$ of Lie
algebras factors as $\fg \to U(\fg) \to A$. (So, for example, any Lie algebra representation of $\fg$ 
factors through an associative algebra representation of $U(\fg)$.)
$U(\fg)$ is typically much bigger than $\fg$.

To define universal enveloping vertex algebras one does similarly.
We first fix a finite-dimensional vector space $R$, with the idea that
each $a \in R$ will give some $a(z)$.
Then we give the singular part of the expansion $Y(a,z) Y(b,w)$, 
i.e. give products $a_{(n)} b$ for $n \ge 0$: this is the analogue of
giving a Lie bracket. We organize them into
the \ti{$\lambda$-bracket} defined by
\begin{equation}
  \lb{a}{\lambda}{b} = \sum_{j \ge 0} \frac{\lambda^j}{j!} a_{(j)} b
\end{equation}
or more analytically
\begin{equation}
  Y(\lb{a}{\lambda}{b},w) = \Res_{z = w} \e^{\lambda(z-w)} Y(a,z) Y(b,w) \, .
\end{equation}
(Here the residue should be understood in the sense of \autoref{thm:vertex-algebra-associativity}.)

Giving this bracket amounts to the following structure:

\begin{defn}[Lie conformal algebra]
A \ti{Lie conformal algebra} is a $\bbC[\partial]$-module $R$
with a $\lambda$-bracket
\begin{equation}
  \lb{\cdot}{\lambda}{\cdot}: R \otimes R \to R[\lambda] \, ,
\end{equation}
expanded as
\begin{equation}
  \lb{a}{\lambda}{b} = \sum_{j \ge 0} \frac{\lambda^j}{j!} a_{(j)} b \, ,
\end{equation}
such that
\begin{gather}
  \lb{\partial a}{\lambda}{b} = -\lambda \lb{a}{\lambda}{b}, \quad \lb{a}{\lambda}{\partial b} = (\lambda + \partial)\lb{a}{\lambda}{b}, \quad \lb{a}{\lambda}{b} = -\lb{b}{-\lambda-\partial}{a} \, , \\
  \lb{a}{\lambda}{\lb{b}{\mu}{c}} - \lb{b}{\mu}{\lb{a}{\lambda}{c}} = \lb{\lb{a}{\lambda}{b}}{\lambda+\mu}{c} \, .
\end{gather}
\end{defn}

Any vertex algebra is a Lie conformal algebra (just as any associative algebra is a Lie algebra),
and we have:

\begin{prop}[Existence of universal enveloping vertex algebra]
Suppose $R$ is a Lie conformal algebra. Then there exists
a vertex algebra $U(R)$ and a morphism $R \to U(R)$ of Lie conformal algebras,
such that for any  
vertex algebra $\vertex$, any morphism
of Lie conformal algebras $R \to \vertex$
factors as $R \to U(R) \to \vertex$.
% there is a unique morphism of vertex algebras
% $\Phi: \vertex(R) \to \vertex'$, such that for all $a \in R$,
% \begin{equation}
%   \Phi(a_{(-1)} \bid) = \phi(a) \, .
% \end{equation}
\end{prop}

Here are three main examples: 
\begin{enumerate}
\item The \ti{Heisenberg algebra} $\vertex = \Heis$. 
Here the Lie conformal algebra is
\begin{equation}
  R = (\bbC[\partial] \cdot J) \oplus (\bbC \cdot \bid)
\end{equation}
where $\partial \bid = 0$.
The $J$-$J$ operator product is
\begin{equation}
 J(z) J(w) = \frac{1}{(z-w)^2} + \regular
\end{equation}
i.e. $\lb{J}{\lambda}{J} = \lambda \bid$, while $\bid$ is central.
% i.e. the only nonzero product is $J_{(1)} J = \bid$.
The mode algebra is spanned by $J_{(n)}$ $(n \in \bbZ)$ and $\bid = \bid_{(-1)}$, with
\begin{equation}
[J_{(m)},J_{(n)}] = m \delta_{m+n,0} \bid \, .
\end{equation}
% Because $Y(J,z) \bid \in J + z \vertex[[z]]$
% we have the relations $J_{(n)} \bid = 0$ for $n \ge 0$.

% As a module over the Lie algebra of modes, $\vertex$
% is freely generated by $\bid$ modulo these relations. Thus
% a basis for $\Heis$ is provided by elements
% $J_{(n_1)} \cdots J_{(n_k)} \bid$ with $n_1 \le n_2 \le \cdots \le n_k < 0$.

\item For any complex Lie algebra $\fg$ with invariant bilinear form $\kappa$ and $k \in \bbC$, 
one has the \ti{affine Kac-Moody algebra} $\vertex = V^k(\fg)$.
Here
\begin{equation}
  R = (\bbC[\partial] \cdot \fg) \oplus (\bbC \cdot \bid)
\end{equation}
with operator products of the form
\begin{equation} \label{eq:kac-moody-ope}
  J^X(z) J^Y(w) = \frac{k \kappa(X,Y)}{(z-w)^2} + \frac{J^{[X,Y]}(w)}{z-w} + \regular
\end{equation}
i.e. $\lb{J^X}{\lambda}{J^Y} = \lambda k \kappa(X,Y) \bid + J^{[X,Y]}$.
The corresponding mode algebra is
\begin{equation}
[J^X_{(m)},J^Y_{(n)}] = J^{[X,Y]}_{(m+n)} + m k \kappa(X,Y) \delta_{m+n,0} \bid \, .
\end{equation}
Note that the zero modes $J^X_{(0)}$ have 
\begin{equation}
  [J^X_{(0)}, J^Y_{(0)}] = J^{[X,Y]}_{(0)}
\end{equation}
so they form a copy of $\fg$ inside $\Lie(\vertex)$.
Also note the Heisenberg vertex algebra is a special case, where $\fg = \fgl_1$ with a single generator $X$,
$\kappa(X,X) = 1$ and $k=1$.

\item For any $c \in \bbC$ one has the \ti{Virasoro algebra} $\Vir^c$. It is generated by a defect $T$, with an expansion of the form
\begin{equation} \label{eq:virasoro-ope}
  T(z) T(w) = \frac{\frac{c}{2}}{(z-w)^4} + \frac{2 T(w)}{(z-w)^2} + \frac{\partial T(w)}{z-w} + \regular
\end{equation}
i.e. $\lb{T}{\lambda}{T} = \lambda^3 \frac{c}{12} \bid + (2 \lambda + \partial) T$.
The corresponding mode Lie algebra is
\begin{equation}
[T_{(m)},T_{(n)}]
=(m-n)\,T_{(m+n-1)}+\frac{c}{12}\,m(m-1)(m-2)\,\delta_{m+n,2} \bid \, ,
\end{equation}
or after defining $L_m = T_{(m+1)}$ to match the usual convention,
\begin{equation}  \label{eq:virasoro-mode-algebra}
  [L_m, L_n] = (m-n) L_{m+n} + \frac{c}{12}\,(m+1)(m)(m-1)\,\delta_{m+n,0} \bid \, .
\end{equation}

\end{enumerate}

\begin{exercise}

from the given $\lb{T}{\lambda}{T}$ and \eqref{eq:mode-algebra}.
\end{exercise}

In all these examples $\vertex = U(R)$ where
$R / (\bbC \cdot \bid)$ is free over $\bbC[\partial]$.
In this situation we have a PBW-type basis:

\begin{prop}[PBW basis for universal enveloping vertex algebra]
Let $R$ be a Lie conformal algebra with a subalgebra $T$ annihilated by $\partial$,
such that $R/T$ is free as a $\bbC[\partial]$-module. Then fix an
ordered $\bbC[\partial]$-basis $(a^i)_{i\in I}$ of $R/T$.
Then the vectors
\[
a^{i_1}_{(-n_1-1)}\,a^{i_2}_{(-n_2-1)}\cdots a^{i_r}_{(-n_r-1)}\,\vac,
\]
with $r\ge 0$, $n_1\ge n_2\ge \cdots \ge n_r\ge 0$, and
$i_k\le i_{k+1}$ whenever $n_k=n_{k+1}$, form a $\bbC$-basis of $U(R)$.

Equivalently, if $\mathfrak L$ is the mode Lie algebra on $R/T$, and
$\mathfrak L_{<0}:=\mathrm{span}\{a_{(n)}:a\in R,\ n<0\}$, then the multiplication map
induces a vector-space isomorphism
\[
U(\mathfrak L_{<0}) \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } U(R),\qquad u\mapsto u\,\vac.
\]

\end{prop}


\begin{example}
Suppose $\vertex = \Heis$. The above theorem says that $\vertex_n$ has a basis indexed by
unordered partitions of $n$, e.g. $\vertex_4$ is spanned by
the vectors
$J_{(-1)}^4 \vac$, $J_{(-2)} J_{(-1)}^2 \vac$, $J_{(-2)}^2 \vac$, $J_{(-3)} J_{(-1)} \vac$, $J_{(-4)} \vac$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Suppose $\vertex = V^k(\fg)$. Then $\vertex_0$ is spanned by $\vac$, $\vertex_1$ is spanned by $J^X_{(-1)} \vac$ for all $X \in \fg$
(i.e. $\vertex_1 \simeq \fg$), 
$\vertex_2$ by $J^X_{(-1)} J^Y_{(-1)} \vac$ and $J^X_{(-2)} \vac$,
and so on.
Each level $\vertex_n$ is a representation of $\fg$, through the
action of the zero modes $J^X_{(0)}$.
$\vertex_0 \simeq \triv$, $\vertex_1 \simeq \fg$, $\vertex_2 \simeq \fg \oplus \Sym^2 \fg$, and so on. 
\end{example}

A useful way to think about this answer, at least informally (for $\vertex = \Heis$): we started out with a single object $J$,
which we thought of as a ``thing which can be inserted at a point $z \in \bbC$''. Then we asked, what are the 
things which appear in the Laurent expansion when we bring two $J$'s close together, and then iterate?
These other things can still be inserted at a point, while still behaving meromorphically with respect to $J$.
The answer includes $J$ but also many other things, all constructed by taking derivatives of $J$, or taking 
contour integrals of one object around another, and iterating. A basis for them is provided by iterated circular 
contour-integrals 
\begin{equation}
  \oint \cdots \oint z_1^{n_1} \cdots z_k^{n_k} J(z_1) \cdots J(z_k) \, .
\end{equation}
It's similar for the other universal enveloping vertex algebras: we can think of them as spanned by iterated
contour-integrals of a set of generating fields.


\subsection{Some vertex operators}

It is interesting to ask what are the vertex
operators $Y(a,z)$ associated to general elements $a \in \vertex$.
We know that
\begin{equation}
  Y(a_{(-1)} \vac, z) = Y(a, z) = \sum_n a_{(n)} z^{-n-1} \, .
\end{equation}
Using the translation axiom we can similarly get
\begin{equation}
  Y(a_{(-m)} \vac, z) = \frac{\partial_z^{m-1}}{(m-1)!} Y(a, z) \, .
\end{equation}

% \begin{prop} \label{prop:goddard-uniqueness}
% If $\phi(z) \in \End(\vertex)[[z]]$, and $\phi(0) \vac = a$,
% and $\phi(z)$ is local with respect to all $Y(b,w)$,
% then $\phi(z) = Y(a,z)$.
% \end{prop}

Now what about something like $Y(J_{(-1)} J_{(-1)} \vac, z)$?
$Y(J_{(-1)} J_{(-1)} \vac, z)$ should 
be a local field which creates the state
$J_{(-1)} J_{(-1)} \vac$ at $z$, i.e. it should be
$\oint_{z} (w-z)^{-1} J(w) J(z) \de w$.
This is an instance of a regularized product between two fields,
called \ti{normal ordered product}:
\begin{defn}[Normally ordered product]
For $a, b \in \vertex$ we define
\begin{equation}
  \nop{a b} = a_{(-1)} b \in \vertex \, .
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

We can also write it in another way:
\begin{multline}
 \nop{J J}(z) = \oint_w (z-w)^{-1} J(z) J(w) \, \de z \\ = \oint_w (z-w)^{-1} \left( J(z) J(w) - \frac{\bid}{(z-w)^2} \right) \, \de z = \lim_{w \to z} \left( J(z) J(w) - \frac{\bid}{(z-w)^2} \right) \, .
\end{multline}
So $\nop{JJ}(z)$ can be obtained by starting
with $J(w) J(z)$, deleting ``by hand'' the singular term $\frac{\bid}{(z-w)^2}$, and taking $w \to z$.

Here is the reason why it is called ``normal ordering'':
\begin{prop}
Define $Y(a,z)_< = \sum_{n < 0} a_{(n)} z^{-n-1}$ and $Y(a,z)_\ge = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_{(n)} z^{-n-1}$.
Then
 \begin{equation}
   Y(\nop{ab}, z) = Y(a,z)_< Y(b,z) + Y(b,z) Y(a,z)_\ge
 \end{equation}
 In terms of modes,
 \begin{equation}
   \nop{ab}_{(n)} = \sum_{k+\ell = n-1} \begin{cases} a_{(k)} b_{(\ell)} & \text{ if } k < 0, \\ b_{(\ell)} a_{(k)} & \text{ if } k \ge 0 \end{cases} 
 \end{equation}
\end{prop}

So, for instance, 
\begin{equation}
\nop{JJ}_{(-1)} = J_{(-1)} J_{(-1)} + 2 J_{(-2)} J_{(0)} + 2 J_{(-3)} J_{(1)} + \cdots 
\end{equation}
Note that this sum actually makes sense in $\End \vertex$, because $J_{(n)} a = 0$ for $n \gg 0$.

The analytic explanation of this formula goes as follows.
As we just discussed we expect to obtain $\nop{ab}(w)$ by expanding $a(z) b(w)$ in $(z-w)$ at fixed $w$,
subtracting all the singular terms, and then setting $z-w = 0$.
Let's see how this works out in Laurent expansions, choosing the region $\abs{z} > \abs{w}$.
Using \autoref{thm:vertex-algebra-associativity} the singular part of $Y(a,z) Y(b,w)$ is the $\abs{z} > \abs{w}$ expansion of
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{n \ge 0} (z-w)^{-n-1} Y(a_{(n)} b, w)
\end{equation}
Now recall that $[Y(a,z), Y(b,w)]$ is the \ti{difference} between the two ways of expanding that
sum. Moreover, the two expansions are easy to distinguish: the 
expansion in $\abs{z} > \abs{w}$ involves only negative powers of $z$,
while the one in $\abs{z} < \abs{w}$ involves only nonnegative powers.
It follows that $[Y(a,z)_\ge,Y(b,w)]$ is the expansion in the region $\abs{z} > \abs{w}$.
In other words, subtracting the singular terms amounts to taking
\begin{equation}
  Y(\nop{ab}, w) = \left( Y(a,z) Y(b,w) - [Y(a,z)_\ge,Y(b,w)] \right) \vert_{z=w}
\end{equation}
which is the desired formula.

Once we know this formula we can use it iteratively to determine all other vertex
operators. For instance:
\begin{exercise}
Calculate the mode expansion of $Y(J_{(-1)} J_{(-1)} J_{(-1)} \vac, z)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Calculate the mode expansion of $Y(J_{(-2)} J_{(-1)} \vac, z)$.
\end{exercise}


\subsection{Modules for vertex algebras}

\begin{defn}[Module for a vertex algebra]
A (weak) \ti{$\vertex$-module} is a vector space $\vrep$
and an operation
\begin{equation}
Y^\vrep(\,\cdot\,,z): \vertex \to \mathrm{End}(\vrep)[[z,z^{-1}]],\qquad
Y^\vrep(a,z) = \sum_{n\in \mathbb{Z}} a^\vrep_{(n)} z^{-n-1},
\end{equation}
such that
\begin{enumerate}

\item For each \(a \in \vertex \), \( \chi \in \vrep \) we have \(a^\vrep_{(n)}\chi=0\) for all \(n\gg 0\) \, ,

\item 
  $Y^\vrep(\vac, z) = \bid_\vrep$ \, ,

\item $Y^\vrep(T a, z) = \partial_z Y^\vrep(a,z)$ \, ,

\item For all $a,b \in \vertex$ and $\chi \in \vrep$
there exists $F(z,w) \in \vrep[[z,w]][z^{-1},w^{-1},(z-w)^{-1}]$, such that
$Y^\vrep(a,z) Y^\vrep(b,w) \chi$ and 
$Y^\vrep(b,w) Y^\vrep(a,z) \chi$ are the
formal Laurent expansion of $F(z,w)$ in the regions
$\abs{w} < \abs{z}$ and $\abs{z} < \abs{w}$
respectively.
\end{enumerate}

\end{defn}

\begin{defn}[Admissible module]
A $\vertex$-module $\vrep$ is \ti{admissible} if
$\vrep = \bigoplus_n \vrep_n$ (warning:
the grading is not necessarily by integers),
where $a_{(n)}^\vrep$ acts with degree $(\deg a) - n - 1$.
\end{defn}

\subsection{Riemann surfaces}

The second main datum of the course is a \ti{Riemann surface} $C$, 
i.e. a complex manifold of complex dimension $1$. 
We usually take $C$ to be compact. 
Two fundamental examples are 
\begin{enumerate}
\item the \ti{Riemann sphere} $C = \bbC \bbP^1$, covered by two patches with coordinates $z$ and $z'$, related by $z' = 1/z$.
\item the \ti{torus} $C = \bbC / (\bbZ \oplus \tau \bbZ)$, depending
on the parameter $\tau \in \bbC$ with $\im \tau > 0$.
\end{enumerate}

We also often include marked points $p_i$ on $C$, each 
equipped with a local coordinate around it. 
Each marked point $p_i$ will be labeled by a $\vertex$-module 
$\vrep_i$.

We often drop the data of the marked points and the representations from the notation, just writing the whole object as $C$.

\section{Chiral correlation functions and conformal blocks}

Now we are ready to ``put $\vertex$ on the Riemann surface $C$.''
The way this is done for a general $\vertex$ takes a while
to appreciate. To get some examples in our hand quickly, 
we will first specialize to the case of affine vertex algebras,
and further to the case of a curve $C$ with no marked points.
Then we will treat more general situations.

Before defining conformal blocks we consider correlation functions.
I think that these are actually the more important objects (and in good
cases they are the same thing as conformal blocks).


\subsection{Chiral correlation functions without marked points}

\begin{defn}[Chiral correlation system for \tops{$V^k(\fg)$}{V^k(g)}, without marked points] \label{defn:chiral-correlation-affine-no-marked}
Suppose given a Riemann surface $C$.
A \ti{chiral correlation system} for $V^k(\fg)$
on $C$ is a rule that assigns an element
\begin{equation}
  \bigg\langle \prod_{j=1}^m J^{X_j}(q_j) \bigg\rangle \in \bigotimes_{j=1}^m T^*_{q_j} C  
\end{equation}
for every $m \ge 0$, $q_j \in C$,
which:
\begin{itemize}
  \item is linear in each $X_i$,
  \item is symmetric under the interchange of the $J^{X_i}(q_i)$,
  \item is meromorphic in the $q_i$, and holomorphic away from
  loci where some $q_i = q_j$,
%   \item has Laurent expansion around $q_j = p_i$ given by
% \begin{equation}
%   \IP{J^X(q) \chi(p) \cdots} = \sum_{n \in \bbZ} z^{-n-1} \IP{(J^X_{(n)} \chi)(p) \cdots} \, ,
% \end{equation}
  \item satisfies the relations \eqref{eq:kac-moody-ope} around $q_i = q_j$, i.e.
  the expansion of
$\IP{J^X(q) J^Y(q') \cdots}$\footnote{Here and below, we use the notation $\cdots$ to mean some
arbitrary allowed insertion in a correlation function; e.g. here $\cdots$ stands for 
an expression of the form $\prod_{i=1}^{m-2} J^{X_i}(q_i)$; in any equation where $\cdots$ appears
multiple times, the understanding is that it stands for the same expression each time.} around $q=q'$ is 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:affine-km-chiral-singularity-condition}
  \IP{J^X(q) J^Y(q') \cdots} = \frac{k \kappa(X,Y) \IP{\cdots} \de z(q) \de z(q')}{(z(q)-z(q'))^2} + \frac{\IP{J^{[X,Y]}(q') \cdots} \de z(q)}{z(q)-z(q')} + \regular \, .
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\end{defn}

\begin{exercise}
Prove that the condition \eqref{eq:affine-km-chiral-singularity-condition} is coordinate-invariant.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove that chiral correlation systems form a vector space over $\bbC$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{example}[Chiral correlation systems for the Heisenberg vertex algebra on the sphere]

Here is a baby example.

Suppose $\vertex = \Heis = V^1(\fgl_1)$, and $C = \bbC \bbP^1$. 
Then we can construct a chiral correlation system as follows.
Let $\sigma$ denote a division of $\{1, \dots, k\}$ into 
2-element subsets $(\sigma_{i1}, \sigma_{i2})$ with $i = 1, \dots, \frac{k}{2}$. Then we can determine a chiral correlation system by
\begin{equation}
  \IP{J(z_1) \cdots J(z_k)} = \sum_\sigma \prod_{i=1}^{\frac{k}{2}} \frac{\de z_{\sigma_{i1}} \de z_{\sigma_{i2}}}{(z_{\sigma_{i1}} - z_{\sigma_{i2}})^2}
\end{equation}
so e.g.
\begin{align}
 \IP{} &= 1, \\
 \IP{J(z_1)} &= 0, \\
 \IP{J(z_1) J(z_2)} &= \frac{1}{(z_1 - z_2)^2} \de z_1 \de z_2, \\
 \IP{J(z_1) J(z_2) J(z_3)} &= 0, \\
 \IP{J(z_1) J(z_2) J(z_3) J(z_4)} &= \left( \frac{1}{(z_1 - z_2)^2 (z_3 - z_4)^2} + \frac{1}{(z_1 - z_3)^2 (z_2 - z_4)^2} + \frac{1}{(z_1 - z_4)^2 (z_2 - z_3)^2}  \right)  \de z_1 \de z_2 \de z_3 \de z_4, 
\end{align}
We can make a diagrammatic representation of this answer as follows.
Let
\[
B(z,w) = \frac{\de z\,\de w}{(z-w)^2}.
\]
Then
\[
\langle J(z_1)\cdots J(z_k)\rangle
=
\sum_{\Gamma} \prod_{\{i,j\}\in E(\Gamma)} B(z_i,z_j),
\]
where the sum is over Feynman diagrams 
$\Gamma$ whose vertices are $\{1,\dots,k\}$ and whose edges form a
\emph{perfect matching} (i.e.\ every vertex has valence $1$).
Here are the three diagrams which contribute when $k=4$.

\end{example}

\begin{example}[Chiral correlation systems for the Heisenberg vertex algebra on the torus]
Take $C = \bbC / (\bbZ \oplus \tau \bbZ)$ and $\vertex=\Heis$. We can construct a chiral 
correlation system depending on a parameter $a \in \bbC$ as follows.
Let $\wp_\tau(z)$ be the Weierstrass function: this is a meromorphic function obeying
\begin{equation}
  \wp_\tau(z + 1) = \wp_\tau(z), \qquad \wp_\tau(z + \tau) = \wp_\tau(z), \qquad \wp(z) = \wp(-z) \, .
\end{equation}
$\wp_\tau$ descends to a function on $C$, with a single pole at $z = 0$, with Laurent expansion
\begin{equation}
  \wp_\tau(z) = \frac{1}{z^2} + \regular \, .
\end{equation}
Let
\[
B(z,w) = \wp_\tau(z-w) \, \de z\,\de w \, , \qquad \eta(z) = a \, \de z \, .
\]
Then
\[
\langle J(z_1)\cdots J(z_k)\rangle
=
\sum_{\Gamma} \prod_{\{i,j\}\in E(\Gamma)} B(z_i,z_j) \prod_{i \in V_0(\Gamma)} \eta(z_i),
\]
where the sum is over Feynman diagrams 
$\Gamma$ whose vertices are $\{1,\dots,k\}$, with every vertex either $0$-valent or $1$-valent. $V_0(\Gamma)$ is the set of 
$0$-valent vertices.

For example,
\begin{align}
\IP{J(z)} &= a \, \de z , \\
 \IP{J(z_1) J(z_2) J(z_3)} &= (a \wp(z_1-z_2) + a \wp(z_1-z_3) + a \wp(z_2-z_3) + a^3) \, \de z_1 \de z_2 \de z_3 \, .
\end{align}
\end{example}

\begin{exercise} Prove that this rule indeed defines
a chiral correlation system.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise} Prove that 
the chiral correlation systems we just described, 
for different values of $a \in \bbC$,
are all linearly independent. Thus in this case the space of 
chiral correlation systems is infinite-dimensional.
\end{exercise}

\begin{example}[Chiral correlation systems for an affine vertex algebra on the sphere]

Here is a construction of chiral correlation functions, originally given in \cite{FrenkelZhu1992}.
\fixme{check factors, signs}
Define
\[
W(i_1, \dots, i_m) =\
\frac{k \, \kappa(X_1,\ad_{X_2}\ad_{X_3}\cdots \ad_{X_{m-1}}(X_m))}{(z_{i_1}-z_{i_2})(z_{i_2}-z_{i_3})\cdots(z_{i_m}-z_{i_1})} \, \de z_{i_1} \cdots \de z_{i_m} \, .
\]
Then the correlator is a sum over decompositions
of $\{1, \dots, n\}$ into disjoint cycles with 
length $m \ge 2$:
\[
\Big\langle \prod_{r=1}^n J^{X_r}(z_r)\Big\rangle
=
\sum_{\{1,\dots,n\} = C_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup C_s}
\ \prod_{\alpha=1}^s \left( \frac{1}{\abs{C_\alpha}} \sum_{\text{orderings }\mathbf{i}\text{ of }C_\alpha} W(\mathbf{i}) \right) \, .
\]
For example,
\begin{equation}
  \IP{J^{X_1}(z_1) J^{X_2}(z_2) J^{X_3}(z_3)} = \frac{k \, \kappa(X_1, [X_2,X_3])}{(z_1 - z_2)(z_2 - z_3)(z_3 - z_1)} \de z_1 \de z_2 \de z_3 \, 
\end{equation}
and
\begin{multline}
  \IP{J^{X_1}(z_1) J^{X_2}(z_2) J^{X_3}(z_3) J^{X_4}(z_4)} = \\ 
  \left( \left( \frac{k \, \kappa(X_1, [X_2, [X_3,X_4]])}{(z_1 - z_2)(z_2 - z_3)(z_3 - z_4)(z_4 - z_1)} + \cdots \right) + \left(  \frac{k^2 \, \kappa(X_1,X_2) \kappa(X_3,X_4)}{(z_1 - z_2)^2 (z_3 - z_4)^2} + \cdots \right) \right) \de z_1 \de z_2 \de z_3 \de z_4 
\end{multline}
where each $\cdots$ is a sum over two similar terms.

\subsection{Including marked points with the vacuum module}

Suppose we have a chiral correlation system for $V^k(\fg)$
as above.
Choose points $p_1, \dots, p_n \in C$ and 
fix a local coordinate $z_i$ centered
at each $p_i$.
Then we can upgrade the correlation functions to permit
arbitrary insertions of elements of $\vertex$, instead of just
$J^X$: i.e. we can consider objects
\begin{equation}
  \IP{ \prod_{r=1}^k J^{X_r}(q_r) \prod_{i=1}^n \cdot(p_i) } :  \left(  \bigotimes_{i=1}^n  \vertex \right) \to \left(  \otimes_{i=1}^k T^*_{q_i} C \right)
\end{equation}
determined by two conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Insertions of $\vac$ can be erased, i.e.
\begin{equation}
  \IP{ \prod_{r=1}^k J^{X_r}(q_r) \prod_{i=1}^n \cdot(p_i) } (\vac, a_2, \dots, a_n) = \IP{ \prod_{r=1}^k J^{X_r}(q_r) \prod_{i=2}^n \cdot(p_i) } (a_2, \dots, a_n)
\end{equation}

  \item 
  The Laurent expansion around $q = p$ is determined by the action
  of $J^X$ on $\vertex$, 
  \begin{equation}
    \IP{ \cdots J^X(q) a(p) } = \sum_n z(q)^{-n-1} \IP{\cdots (J^X_{(n)} a)(p) }
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

  By contour-integration this formula determines the terms on the RHS. So this gives a way of inductively constructing up these
  correlation functions starting from the chiral correlation system.
  There are two extreme cases:
  \begin{itemize}
   \item $n=0$ recovers the original chiral correlation system;
  \item  $k=0$ gives a map $\left( \otimes_{i=1}^n \vertex  \right) \to \bbC$. That data-type doesn't involve the Riemann surface $C$, but $C$ still enters implicitly, through constraints which this linear map obeys.
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}
Take $C = \bbC\bbP^1$. Use the standard
coordinates $z$ and $w = 1/z$, and choose the marked points
to be $z = 0$ and $w = 0$, i.e. north and south pole.
Then the chiral correlation system we discussed above 
gives a map 
$\Phi: \vertex \otimes \vertex \to \bbC$. What is that map?
First, we have 
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(\vac, \vac) = \IP{1} = 1 \, . 
\end{equation}
Second, by contour-deformation we have\footnote{Many authors consider the ``Shapovalov form'' 
$\Phi_S$ on $\vertex$, which is slightly different.
With respect to $\Phi_S$ the adjoint of $J^X_{(n)}$ is $J^{\omega(X)}_{(-n)}$, where $\omega$ is an anti-involution of $\fg$ (note this anti-involution is not $\fg$-invariant, so it could not have arisen from our construction, which so far
has been fully $\fg$-invariant). The two are related by
$\Phi_S(a,b) = \Phi(a,\omega b)$, where $\omega$ now is the 
corresponding involution of $\vertex$, which is
self-adjoint for $\Phi$.}
\begin{equation}
\Phi(J^X_{(n)} a, b) = \oint_{z=0} z^n \IP{b(\infty) J^X(z) a(0)} = - \oint_{w=0} w^{-n} \IP{b(\infty) J^X(w) a(0)} = - \Phi(a, J^X_{(-n)} b).
\end{equation}
(Or equivalently, the sum of residues of the global meromorphic $1$-form $\IP{z^n b(\infty) J^X(z) a(0)}$ vanishes.)

These two facts are enough to iteratively determine $\Phi$.
For example, on the grade-$1$ subspace:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(J^X_{(-1)} \vac, J^Y_{(-1)} \vac) = -\Phi(\vac, J^X_{(1)} J^Y_{(-1)} \vac) = -\Phi(\vac, k \kappa (X,Y) \vac) = -k \kappa(X,Y) \, .
\end{equation}
% $\Phi$ is a symmetric bilinear form on $\vertex$.

Given $\Phi$ we could actually reconstruct the 
whole chiral correlation system, by summing up Laurent
series, e.g.
\begin{equation}
  \IP{J^X(z) J^Y(w)} = \sum_{n,m \in \bbZ} z^{-n-1} w^{-m-1} \Phi(\vac, J^X_{(n)} J^Y_{(m)} \vac) \text{ for } \abs{z} > \abs{w} \, . 
\end{equation}
(So this gives an alternate way of computing
the chiral correlation system on $\bbC \bbP^1$, 
and seeing that it is unique up to scale.)

Now we could also insert a third marked point, at
$z$. Then the chiral correlation function
gives $\Phi_{3,z}: \vertex \otimes \vertex \otimes \vertex \to \bbC$.
Its Laurent expansion in $z$ is
$\Phi_{3,z}(a,b,c) = \Phi(a, Y(b,z) c)$.
In this sense $\Phi_3$ is closely related to the
vertex algebra operation. We would be tempted to say they
are equivalent, if $\Phi$ is nondegenerate.

This raises the interesting question of whether $\Phi$
is actually nondegenerate or not. It is ``easy'' to show
that the graded components of $\vertex$ are orthogonal
under $\Phi$. Also for $\fg = \fgl_1$ (Heisenberg)
the partition basis is an orthogonal basis.

How about $\fg = \fsl_2$? Here we can fix a PBW basis 
and compute the 
determinant $\Delta_n$ of the matrix of $\Phi$ at each grade $n$.
At the first few orders the result is:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_1 \propto k^3, \quad \Delta_2 \propto k^9(k-1)^5(k+2), \quad \Delta_3 \propto k^{22}(k-1)^{15}(k-2)^7(k+2)^4(3k+4)^3, ...
\end{equation}
So there are some special values of $k$ where $\Phi$ 
is degenerate: in these formulas we see already that this
happens at $k = 1, 2, -2, -\frac34$.
The complete answer is that $\Phi$ is degenerate iff
$k = -2 + p/q$ for some $p \ge 2$, $q > 0$, and $(p,q) = 1$. 
(So in particular, $\Phi$ is degenerate if $k \in \bbZ_{\ge 0}$.)

If $\Phi$ is degenerate, then we are in an 
interesting situation: it means there are some elements
of $\vertex$ which ``behave like zero'' in all correlation
functions on $\bbC \bbP^1$. These elements
form an ideal $I$ in $\vertex$ (meaning $I$ is stable
under all $Y(a,z)$), in fact the unique maximal ideal. 
Then we can form a quotient $L_k(\fg) = \vertex/I$, 
which is a simple vertex algebra, i.e. it has no nontrivial ideals.
If $\Phi$ is nondegenerate then $V^k(\fg)$ is already
simple.

\end{example}


\begin{exercise} Prove that the ideal $I$ is the unique maximal ideal in $\vertex$.
\end{exercise}

\end{exercise}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise} Calculate the determinant $\Delta_2$. (This seems tedious, but maybe fun to do once?)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise} Suppose given any unpunctured curve $C$ and any chiral correlation system for $V^k(\fg)$.
Then fix a point $p \in C$ and a coordinate system $z$ around $p$,
and consider the corresponding $1$-point correlation function $\Phi: \vertex \to \bbC$.
Letting $p$ vary this gives a function $\Phi_z: \vertex \to \bbC$.
Prove that $\partial_z \Phi_z(a) = \Phi_z(T a)$.
\end{exercise}


\subsection{Including other modules}

Next we want to upgrade all this again to include more general $\vertex$-modules.
So let us first quickly discuss some important examples of modules for $V^k(\fg)$.
Let $\hfg$ be the Lie algebra generated by all modes $J^X_{(n)}$ (called \ti{affine Kac-Moody algebra}).
The representation theory of $V^k(\fg)$ is closely related to that of $\hfg$.
For the latter the book \cite{Kac1990InfiniteDimensionalLieAlgebras} is very useful. Here we
have to be brief.

Here is an important class of modules for
$\vertex = V^k(\fg)$.
\begin{prop}
Suppose $\vrep$ is a \ti{smooth} $\hfg$-module: this means 
$\bid \in \hat\fg$ acts as the identity
and, for all $\chi$, $J^X_{(n)} \chi = 0$ for $n \gg 0$.
Then, $\vrep$ is also a $\vertex$-module.
\end{prop}
(The action of $Y(J^X, z)$ comes directly from the $\hat\fg$-action; 
more general fields are determined by iterated contour-integration
as usual.)

\begin{example}[Parabolic Verma modules] \label{exa:parabolic-verma-module}
Fix a $\fg$-module $M$. Let
$\hfg_{\ge 0}$ act on $M$ as follows:
the modes $X_{(0)}$ act through the $\fg$-action on $M$,
and the positive modes $X_{(n)}$ act by $0$.
Then let 
$\vrep = \Ind^{\hfg}_{\hfg_{\ge 0}} M$.\footnote{Recall the definition: for any $\fh \subset \fg$ (or more generally
any map of Lie algebras $\fh \to \fg$), and any $\fh$-module $M$, we get a $\fg$-module $\Ind_\fh^\fg M = U(\fg) \otimes_{U(\fh)} M$.}
It is graded, $\vrep = \bigoplus_{n \in \bbN} \vrep_n$, with $\vrep_0 = M$.

The case $M = \triv$ gives $\vrep = \vertex$ (called the \ti{vacuum module}.)
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Irregular modules] \label{exa:irregular-module}
Fix an element $u \in \fg^*$.
Then let $\hfg_{\ge 1}$ act on $\bbC$
as follows: the modes $X_{(1)}$ act by $u(X)$,
all higher modes $X_{(n)}$ act by $0$.
Then let $\vrep = \Ind^{\hfg}_{\hfg_{\ge 1}} \bbC$.
This one is a little more exotic than the previous: in particular it is not graded,
because it contains a vector $\vacu$ with $X_{(1)} \vacu = u(X) \vacu$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Hybrid modules]
Again fix an element $u \in \fg^*$, let $\fc = Z(u)$ (the centralizer),
and choose a $\fc$-module $M$.
Then consider the subalgebra $\hfg_{\ge 1} \oplus \hfc_{0}$.
This subalgebra acts on $M$ as follows:
the modes $X_{(0)}$ for $X \in \fc$ act through the $\fc$-action on $M$,
the modes $X_{(1)}$ act by $u(X) \bid_M$,
all higher modes $X_{(n)}$ act by $0$.
(To check this is actually a Lie algebra action, we need to use the fact that $[\fg,\fc] \subset \ker u$.)
Then let $\vrep = \Ind^{\hfg}_{\hfg_{\ge 1} + \hfc_{\ge 0}} M$.
This specializes to the previous two cases, upon choosing $u = 0$ or $M = \triv$.
(Note that if $u$ is a regular element then $\fc = 0$, so this generalization is not interesting in that case:
we only get interesting hybrid modules when $0 \subsetneqq Z(u) \subsetneqq \fg$.)
\end{example}




Suppose we have
$\vertex$-modules $\vrep_i$ and insert them at 
marked points $p_i \in C$, still with a local coordinate
system $z_i$ fixed around each $p_i$. Then:

\begin{defn}[Chiral correlation system]
A chiral correlation system for $V^k(\fg)$ on $C$ (where $C$ now includes the marked points $p_i$, local coordinates
$z_i$ and modules $\vrep_i$) is a system of ``functions''
\begin{equation}
  \IP{ \prod_{r=1}^k J^{X_r}(q_r) \prod_{i=1}^n \cdot(p_i) } :  \left(  \bigotimes_{i=1}^n  \vrep_i \right) \to \left(  \otimes_{i=1}^k T^*_{q_i} C \right)
\end{equation}
obeying all the conditions in \autoref{defn:chiral-correlation-affine-no-marked}, plus
a Laurent-expansion condition as $q_i \to p_j$,
\begin{equation}
    \IP{ \cdots J^X(q) \chi(p) } = \sum_{n \in \bbZ} z(q)^{-n-1} \IP{\cdots (J^{X,\vrep}_{(n)} \chi)(p) } \, .
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

Note that, in contrast to the case where all $\vrep_i = \vertex$, 
here having chiral correlation
system without marked points does \ti{not} 
automatically construct for us
a chiral correlation system with marked points.

\begin{example}
Suppose again that $C = \bbC \bbP^1$ and we insert $\vertex$-modules $\vrep$, $\vrep'$ at $0$ and $\infty$, with local 
coordinates as before.

Then, by the same method as before, 
a chiral correlation system on $C$ gives in particular 
a pairing $\Phi: \vrep \otimes \vrep' \to \bbC$
obeying again the condition
$\Phi(J^X_{(n)} \chi, \psi) = - \Phi(\chi, J^X_{(-n)} \psi)$.
Such a pairing might or might not exist, depending on
what $\vrep$ and $\vrep'$ are.

For instance, suppose $\vrep = \Ind^{\hfg}_{\hfg_{\ge 0}} M$ as in 
\autoref{exa:parabolic-verma-module}, and similarly $\vrep'$.
Then we can restrict attention to the zero-grade parts 
$M$, $M'$, which are closed under the action of the zero-modes $J^X_{(0)}$.
The invariance condition 
$\Phi(J^X_{(0)} \chi, \psi) = - \Phi(\chi, J^X_{(0)} \psi)$
just says that $\Phi$ gives a $\fg$-invariant pairing $M \otimes M' \to \bbC$.
Moreover, similarly to the case we discussed above, the full $\Phi$ is determined (and not overdetermined)
by its restriction to $M \otimes M'$, and once we have $\Phi$ we can sum Laurent series to build
a chiral correlation system.
The conclusion is that chiral correlation systems on $C$ correspond to $\fg$-invariant pairings $M \otimes M' \to \bbC$.
In particular, if $M$ and $M'$ are irreducible, then the space of chiral correlation systems has dimension $1$ if
$M' \simeq M^*$, and dimension $0$ otherwise.

Another interesting example: suppose $\vrep = \Ind^{\hfg}_{\hfg_{\ge 0}} M$ as above, but
$\vrep'$ is an irregular module as in \autoref{exa:irregular-module}. In this case by a similar analysis
we conclude that a chiral correlation system is uniquely determined by the values $\Phi(m \otimes \vacu)$ for $m \in M$,
and these values are unconstrained. Thus the space of chiral correlation systems as a vector space is $M^*$. 
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Now suppose that again that $C = \bbC \bbP^1$ and we insert arbitrary $\vertex$-modules $\vrep_i$ at marked points $p_i$,
with arbitrary local coordinates $z_i$.

If we have a chiral correlation system, then as before, we get a map
\begin{equation}
 \Phi: \bigotimes_{i=1}^n \vrep_i \to \bbC \, . 
\end{equation}

This map is subject to many conditions. To get the simplest of these conditions we consider
the 1-form $\eta(z) = \IP{J^X(z) \prod_{i=1}^n \chi_i(p_i)}$. This 1-form is meromorphic, with singularities only at the $p_i$,
on the compact Riemann surface $C$, so the sum of its residues must be zero. That gives the condition
\begin{equation} \label{eq:coinvariant-condition-zero-modes}
  \sum_{i=1}^n \Phi(\chi_1, \dots, J^{X,\vrep_i}_{(0)} \chi_i, \dots, \chi_n) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
More generally we can consider the 1-form $f(z) \eta(z)$, where $f$ is any meromorphic function on $C$ which is holomorphic
away from the $p_i$. Then we get a more general condition
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^n \Res_{p_i} (f \eta) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
So what are these residues? Laurent expand near each $p_i$ as
\begin{equation}
  f = \sum_{m \in \bbZ} f_{i,m}\, z_i^{m} \, .
\end{equation}
Then the condition is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:coinvariant-condition-concrete}
  \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{m \in \bbZ} f_{i,m} \Phi(\chi_1, \dots, J^{X,\vrep_i}_{(m)} \chi_i, \dots, \chi_n) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
Conversely, given $\Phi$ which obeys \eqref{eq:coinvariant-condition-concrete} for every $f$, we can build a chiral correlation system.
(This is a nontrivial claim: it says that the Laurent series we build out of $\Phi$ actually converge! \fixme{...})
Another way to phrase the condition \eqref{eq:coinvariant-condition-concrete}: 
there is a linear subspace $K \subset W = \otimes_i \vrep_i$ 
consisting of vectors of the form 
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{m \in \bbZ} \chi_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes (f_{i,m} J^{X,\vrep_i}_{(m)}) \chi_i \otimes \cdots \otimes \chi_n
\end{equation}
and $\Phi$ must annihilate $K$.

Now how big is $K$? Let's consider the case 
where $\vrep_i = \Ind^{\hfg}_{\hfg_{\ge 0}} M_i$.
First note that for any $i$ and any $m < 0$, the function $f(z) = (z - z_i)^m$
has $f_{i,m} = 1$ and all other $f_{j,m} = 0$ for $m<0$.
It follows that $K \oplus W_0 = W$. Next note that any element in $S \cap W_0$ must
come from a constant function $f$ (since these are the only functions on $\bbC \bbP^1$ which has
$f_{i,m} = 0$ for all $m < 0$).
It follows that any $\Phi_0: W_0 \to \bbC$ which obeys the condition
\eqref{eq:coinvariant-condition-zero-modes} has a unique extension to
$\Phi: W \to \bbC$ obeying \eqref{eq:coinvariant-condition-concrete}.
Said otherwise, the space of chiral correlation systems on $C$ is
\begin{equation}
  \Hom^\fg(\bigotimes_{i=1}^n M_i, \triv) \, .
\end{equation}

\end{example}


Our last discussion motivates the following:

\begin{defn}[Conformal block for affine algebra]
Suppose $C$ is a Riemann surface with marked points $p_i$, local coordinates $z_i$, and $V^k(\fg)$-modules $\vrep_i$.
A \ti{conformal block} for $V^k(\fg)$ on $C$ is a linear map $\Phi: \bigotimes_i \vrep_i \to \bbC$ which annihilates
all vectors of the form
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{m \in \bbZ} \chi_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes (f_{i,m} J^{X,\vrep_i}_{(m)}) \chi_i \otimes \cdots \otimes \chi_n
\end{equation}
where $f$ is any global meromorphic function on $C$, with poles only at $p_i$.
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}[Conformal blocks vs. chiral correlation systems] Fix $C$ 
with markings as above. By Fourier expansion we obtain a linear map from the space of chiral correlation systems
for $V^k(\fg)$ on $C$ to the space of conformal blocks. This map is injective if $C$ is connected and has at least
one marked point.
\end{prop}

For a completely arbitrary conformal block it is not clear that summing up the formal Laurent series will give 
an actual function.
As a piece of personal philosophy: the \ti{most interesting} conformal blocks are the ones which actually come
from chiral correlation systems.


\subsection{Changes of coordinate, by hand}

Given a chiral correlation system for $V^k(\fg)$ and \ti{any} choice of marked points with local coordinate systems,
we found a corresponding conformal block $\Phi: \otimes_i \vertex \to \bbC$. Now we can ask: what is the relation
between these blocks for different choices of marked points and coordinates?
We already know the answer, in a sense: we built into our definition of chiral correlation systems
that the $J^X$ are global 1-forms; and all the other elements of $\vertex$
are built out of $J^X$; so their transformation laws are all in principle determined. 
But let us try to spell this out a bit more.

Let $\cG$ denote the group of changes of coordinates on the formal disc.
The elements of $\cG$ are formal power series $a_1 z + a_2 z^2 + \cdots$ with $a_1 \neq 0$,
which multiply by formal composition. Then there is an action $\rho: \cG \to \End \vertex$, 
which is compatible with the vertex algebra structure in the sense that
\begin{equation}
  \rho(g) Y(a,z) \rho(g)^{-1} = Y(\rho(g) a, g(z)) \, .
\end{equation}

% and (we will see) 
% the different functionals $\Phi$ differ by precomposition with this action.

We start with the infinitesimal version, i.e.
consider the \ti{Witt algebra}, the 
Lie algebra $\fG$ of vector fields on the formal disc which
vanish at the origin.
If $\ell_n$ denotes the vector field $- z^{n+1} \partial_z$ then we have for all $n, m \ge 0$
\begin{equation} \label{eq:witt-algebra}
  [\ell_n, \ell_m] = (n-m) \ell_{n+m} \, .
\end{equation}
To figure out what action we should write, take
\begin{equation}
  J^X_{(m)} = \oint z^m J^X(z)  
\end{equation}
and then consider the effect of $z^{n+1} \partial_z$: it acts only on the explicit factor $z^m$,
giving
\begin{equation} \label{eq:witt-action-on-affine}
  [\ell_n, J^X_{(m)}] = -\oint z^{n+1} \partial_z z^m J^X(z) = -m J^X_{(m+n)} \, . 
\end{equation}
Combining this with $\ell_n \vac = 0$ determines an action of $\fG$ on $\vertex$.
It follows directly from our iterated contour-integral construction that 
the conformal blocks we construct are compatible with this action:
\begin{prop}[Coordinate covariance of conformal blocks, with the vacuum module]
Fix a chiral correlation system on a curve $C$.
For any choice of local coordinates $z_i$ at marked points $p_i$, let
\[
\Phi_{(z_i)} : \bigotimes_{i=1}^n \vertex \to \bbC
\]
be the conformal block obtained from the correlation system.
If we replace the local coordinates $z_i$ by
\[
\tilde z_i = g_i(z_i)\qquad (i=1,\dots,n),
\]
then the corresponding block is given by
\[
\Phi_{(\tilde z_i)}
= \Phi_{(z_i)} \circ \bigl(\rho(g_1)\otimes\cdots\otimes \rho(g_n)\bigr) \, .
\]
% i.e.
% \[
% \Phi_{(\tilde z_i)}(v_1\otimes\cdots\otimes v_n)
% =
% \Phi_{(z_i)}\bigl(\rho(g_1)v_1\otimes\cdots\otimes \rho(g_n)v_n\bigr).
% \]
% Equivalently, the assignment $(z_i)\mapsto \Phi_{(z_i)}$ is $\cG^n$-equivariant.
\end{prop}

In a similar fashion we can define compatibility for a $\cG$-action on a $\vrep$-module:
this means we require
$\rho^\vrep: \cG \to \End \vrep$ with
\begin{equation}
  \rho^\vrep(g) Y^\vrep(a,z) \rho^\vrep(g)^{-1} = Y^\vrep(\rho(g) a, g(z)) \, .
\end{equation}

\begin{prop}[Coordinate covariance of conformal blocks, with more general modules] 
Suppose $\vrep_i$ are $\vertex$-modules, with a compatible $\cG$-action.
Suppose $C$ is a Riemann surface with marked points $p_i$ and local coordinates $z_i$, and
\begin{equation}
  \Phi: \bigotimes_{i=1}^n \vrep_i \to \bbC
\end{equation}
is a conformal block.
If we define
\[
\tilde z_i = g_i(z_i)\qquad (i=1,\dots,n),
\]
then
\[
\Phi_{(\tilde z_i)}
= \Phi_{(z_i)} \circ \bigl(\rho(g_1)\otimes\cdots\otimes \rho(g_n)\bigr) \, .
\]
is also a conformal block, now with the local coordinates $\tilde z_i$.
\end{prop}


\subsection{Chiral correlation systems and conformal blocks for vertex algebras with \tops{$\cG$}{G}-action}

Using this we can finally define chiral correlation systems and conformal blocks, now for any vertex algebra
with a compatible $\cG$-action, instead of just $\vertex = V^k(\fg)$.  
There are variants depending on how we treat the coordinate
systems around the marked points.

\begin{defn}[Vector bundles associated to $\vertex$-modules] For $p \in C$ and $\vrep$ a $\vertex$-module with compatible $\cG$-action, let $F_p$ be the set of
formal local coordinate systems around $p$, and
\begin{equation}
\vrep_p = \vrep \times_{\cG} F_p \, .
\end{equation}
\end{defn}
(In particular we can apply this when $\vrep = \vertex$.)
If $\vrep$ is graded, then
the spaces $\vrep_p$ are fibers of an ind-vector bundle 
over $C$: roughly this means a quasicoherent sheaf which is 
filtered by holomorphic (finite-rank) vector bundles (note that the action of $\cG$ on
$\vrep$ does not preserve the graded components $\vrep_n$ 
but does preserve the filtration $\vrep_{\le n}$.)

The fibers $\vrep_p$ can be identified with $\vrep$, but not canonically: one gets an identification only
after choosing an element in $F_p$, i.e. a local coordinate around $z$.
We can use them to define chiral correlation systems in a way that is independent of the local coordinate:

\begin{defn}[Chiral correlation systems for a vertex algebra and modules with $\cG$-action]
Suppose $\vertex$ is a vertex algebra with a compatible $\cG$-action, and $C$ is a Riemann surface, with marked points $p_i$, and $\vertex$-modules $\vrep_i$
with compatible $\cG$-actions. 
Then a \ti{chiral correlation system} is a collection
\begin{equation}
  \IP{ \prod_{i=1}^n a_i(q_i) \prod_{i=1}^k \chi_i(p_i) } \in \bbC
\end{equation}
where each $a_i \in \vertex_{q_i}$, $\chi_i \in \vrep_{p_i}$, such that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The $\IP{\cdots}$ are symmetric in the $q_i$.
  \item The $\IP{\cdots}$ are multilinear in the $a_i$ and $\chi_i$.
  \item The $\IP{\cdots}$ vary meromorphically with each $q_i$.
  \item Fix $s$ either $p_i$ or $q_i$, and choose a local coordinate $z$ around $s$.
  We describe the singular part of $\IP{\cdots}$ as $q \to s$.
  Let $\vrep$ be the $\vertex$-module corresponding to $s$.
  Given $a \in \vertex$, and $q$ near $s$,
  use the local coordinate $z$ to produce $a(q) \in \vrep_q$.
  Then, for any $\chi \in \vrep$, we have
  \begin{equation}
    \IP{ \cdots a(q) \chi(s) } = \sum_{n \in \bbZ} z(q)^{-n-1} \IP{\cdots (a_{(n)} \chi)(p) } \, .
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
\end{defn}

Likewise we can define conformal blocks in a way that is independent of the local coordinate:

\begin{defn}[Conformal blocks for a vertex algebra and modules with $\cG$-action]
Suppose $\vertex$ is a vertex algebra with $\cG$-action.
Suppose $C$ is a Riemann surface with marked points $p_i$, and $\vertex$-modules $\vrep_i$ with compatible $\cG$-action.
Let $\vertex(C)$ denote the space of global meromorphic sections of the vertex algebra bundle 
over $C$, with poles only at the $p_i$.
Fix a local coordinate $z_i$ around each $p_i$, and
for any $a \in \vertex(C)$
write its local expansion around $p_i$ as $\sum_{m \in \bbZ} a_{i,m} z_i^{m}$, with each $a_{i,m} \in \vertex$.
Then, for any $a \in \vertex(C)$, we have the operator
\begin{equation}
  o(a) \in \End \bigotimes_i (\vrep_i)_{p_i} 
\end{equation}
given by
\begin{equation}
  o(a) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{m \in \bbZ} \bid \otimes \cdots \otimes (a_{i,m})_{(m)} \otimes \cdots \otimes \bid
\end{equation}
where we use the local coordinate $z_i$ to identify $\vrep_i$ with $(\vrep_i)_{p_i}$.
Define the subspace
\begin{equation} \label{eq:K-subspace}
 K(C) = \Span \{ \im o(a) \mid  a \in \vertex(C) \}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \Coinv(C) = \left( \bigotimes_i (\vrep_i)_{p_i} \right) / K(C) \, .
\end{equation}
Then a \ti{conformal block} for $\vertex$ on $C$ is a linear map 
\begin{equation}
 \Phi: \Coinv(C) \to \bbC \, . 
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

In the case $\vertex = V^k(\fg)$ this recovers our earlier definition of conformal blocks by coinvariants.

\begin{exercise}

(Hint: if $\tilde z_i = g_i(z_i)$ then the identification of $(\vrep_i)_{p_i}$ with $\vrep_i$ changes by $\rho(g_i)$. Check that the local mode expansions are conjugated accordingly, so the span of the $\im o(a)$ is unchanged.)
\end{exercise}


There is also a variant of all the above where we consider only an action of the subgroup $\fG_+$,
consisting of power series with $a_1 = 1$, i.e. $z + a_2 z^2 + a_3 z^3 + \cdots$ In this case we cannot completely eliminate the coordinate dependence, but we can reduce it to a dependence
on the $1$-jet of the coordinate, or dually a nonzero 
tangent vector at the marked point.

\subsection{Changes of coordinate, by Virasoro}

Now let us reconsider the $\cG$-action on $V^k(\fg)$ from a different point of view.
We specialize very slightly to the case $k \neq - h^\vee$.
The idea is to 
find a copy of $\fG$ inside $\vertex$, and then use the general fact that 
$\vertex$ acts on itself. For this consider $\omega \in \vertex$ defined by
\begin{equation}
  \omega = \frac{1}{2(k + h^\vee)} \sum_i J^{X^i}_{(-1)} J^{X_i}_{(-1)} \vac
\end{equation}
where $X^i$, $X_i$ are dual bases of $\fg$ with respect to $\kappa$,
and $h^\vee$ is the dual Coxeter number of $\fg$ (see \autoref{sec:lie-conventions}).
The key fact about $\omega$ is:
\begin{prop}[Sugawara construction] The $\lambda$-bracket of $\omega$ with itself and with $J^X$ are
\begin{equation} \label{eq:omega-lambda-bracket}
\lb{\omega}{\lambda}{\omega} = \frac{c}{12} \lambda^3 \bid + (2 \lambda + \partial) \omega  , \qquad \lb{\omega}{\lambda}{J^X} = (\partial + \lambda) J^X \, .
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  c = \frac{k \dim \fg}{k + h^\vee} \, .
\end{equation}
\end{prop}
In principle one could prove this directly by hand, carefully manipulating the singular parts of the OPEs to figure
out what singularity we get when we bring $\omega(z)$ close to $\omega(w)$.
But it seems a bit hairy to do so. It might be better to introduce the ``noncommutative Wick formula''\footnote{I had trouble chasing down a good reference for this formula; \cite{kac1998vertex} seems to give a version for the mode expansions; \cite{DeSoleKac2006FiniteVsAffineW} gives the formula as we wrote it here. Warning: \cite{Arakawa2016IntroductionWAlgebras} has a slightly different formula, hopefully a typo there.}
\begin{lem}[Noncommutative Wick formula]
\begin{equation} \label{eq:nc-wick}
\lb{a}{\lambda}{\nop{bc}} = \nop{\lb{a}{\lambda}{b} c} + \nop{b \lb{a}{\lambda}{c}} + \int_0^\lambda \lb{\lb{a}{\lambda}{b}}{\mu}{c} \, \de \mu \, .
\end{equation}
\end{lem}
This lemma is an efficient way of keeping track of the various singular terms which appear when we bring
composite objects together.
To get an idea of the proof of the lemma we can use successive contour-integration: indeed the two operations
$\nop{ab}$ and $\lb{a}{\lambda}{b}$ are both defined by contour-integrals.
The left side of \eqref{eq:nc-wick} can be written as \fixme{make figure with contours}
\begin{equation}
  \oint_x \de z \, \e^{\lambda (z-x)} a(z) \left(  \oint_x \, \frac{\de w}{w-x} b(w) c(x) \right)
\end{equation}
By deformation of the $z$ contour this turns into
\begin{equation}
  \oint_x \frac{\de w}{w-x} \left(  \oint_w \de z \, \e^{\lambda (z-x)} a(z) b(w) \right) c(x) + \oint_x \frac{\de w}{w-x} \left(  \oint_x \de z \, \e^{\lambda (z-x)} a(z) b(w) \right) c(x)  \, .
\end{equation}
The second term is $\nop{b \lb{a}{\lambda}{c}}$, while the first differs from
$\nop{\lb{a}{\lambda}{b} c}$ by the error term
\begin{equation} \label{eq:nc-wick-error-term}
  \oint_x \frac{\de w}{w-x} \left(  \oint_w \de z \, [\e^{\lambda (z-x)}-\e^{\lambda (z-w)}] a(z) b(w) \right) c(x) \, .
\end{equation}
We can re-expand the function appearing here as
\begin{equation}
\frac{\e^{\lambda(z-x)} - \e^{\lambda(z-w)}}{w-x} = \e^{\lambda(z-w)} \int_0^\lambda \de \mu \, \e^{\mu(w-x)}
\end{equation}
and thus recognize the error term \eqref{eq:nc-wick-error-term} as
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^\lambda \de \mu \left[ \oint_x \de w \, \e^{\mu(w-x)} \left( \oint_w \de z \, \e^{\lambda(z-w)} a(z) b(w) \right) c(x) \right]
\end{equation}
which is
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^\lambda \de \mu \, \lb{\lb{a}{\lambda}{b}}{\mu}{c}
\end{equation}
as desired.

Now to prove \eqref{eq:omega-lambda-bracket} we work in a $\kappa$-orthonormal basis $(X^i)$ for $\fg$,
use $J^i$ for $J^{X^i}$, and write $[X^i,X^j] = f^{ijk} X^k$, with the convention that we always
sum over repeated indices. Then \eqref{eq:nc-wick} gives
\begin{equation}
  \lb{J^i}{\lambda}{\nop{J^j J^j}} = \nop{\lb{J^i}{\lambda}{J^j}J^j} + \nop{J^j\lb{J^i}{\lambda}{J^j}} + \int_0^\lambda \lb{\lb{J^i}{\lambda}{J^j}}{\mu}{J^j} \, \de \mu \, ,
\end{equation}
and recalling $\lb{J^k}{\lambda}{J^\ell} = \lambda k \delta^{k \ell} \bid + f^{k\ell m} J^m$ this is
\begin{equation}
  = \nop{(\lambda k \delta^{ij} \bid + f^{ijm} J^m) J^j} + \nop{J^j(\lambda k \delta^{ij} \bid + f^{ijm} J^m)} + \int_0^\lambda \lb{(\lambda k \delta^{ij} \bid + f^{ijm} J^m)}{\mu}{J^j} \, \de \mu \, ,
\end{equation}
which simplifies to
\begin{equation}
  = 2 \lambda k J^i + f^{ijm} (\nop{J^m J^j} + \nop{J^j J^m}) + \int_0^\lambda f^{ijm} f^{mj\ell} J^\ell \, \de \mu \, .
\end{equation}
Now using the asymmetry of $f^{ijm}$ and $f^{ijm} f^{mj\ell} = 2 h^\vee \delta^i_\ell$ (see \autoref{eq:f-coxeter}), 
this simplifies to
\begin{equation}
 \lb{J^i}{\lambda}{\nop{J^j J^j}} = 2 (k + h^\vee) \lambda J^i
\end{equation}
and using the skew-symmetry property of $\lambda$-brackets this is also
\begin{equation}
 \lb{\nop{J^j J^j}}{\lambda}{J^i} = 2 (k + h^\vee) (\lambda+\partial) J^i \, .
\end{equation}
Then dividing out the factor $2 (k + h^\vee)$ we get the second
equation in \eqref{eq:omega-lambda-bracket}.
Next we want to compute $\lb{\omega}{\lambda}{\omega}$;
for that we use \eqref{eq:nc-wick} again to 
reduce $\lb{\nop{J^i J^i}}{\lambda}{\nop{J^j J^j}}$ to things
we have already computed.

\end{exercise}

The equations \eqref{eq:omega-lambda-bracket} are extremely important.
The first equation in \eqref{eq:omega-lambda-bracket} is the $\lambda$-bracket of the generator of the 
Virasoro algebra. It implies that there is a homomorphism of vertex algebras, $\Vir^c \to V^k(\fg)$.
Also if we write
\begin{equation}
  Y(\omega,z) = \sum L_n z^{-n-2}
\end{equation}
(note the shifted index: $L_n = \omega_{(n+1)}$)
then we have
\begin{equation}
  [L_m, L_n] = (m-n) L_{m+n} + \frac{c}{12}\,(m+1)(m)(m-1)\,\delta_{m+n,0} \bid \, ,
\end{equation}
which is the Virasoro Lie algebra.
\eqref{eq:omega-lambda-bracket} also implies
\begin{equation} \label{eq:virasoro-affine-bracket}
  [L_n, J^X_m] = -m J^X_{m+n} \, .
\end{equation}

\begin{example} If $\fg = \fsl_2$ then $h^\vee = 2$, so $c = \frac{3k}{k+2}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example} If $\fg = \fgl_1$ then $h^\vee = 0$, so $c = 1$, independent of $k$.
\end{example}

Now if we consider only the modes $L_n$ with $n \ge 0$, the central charge $c$ does not appear, and
we just see the Witt algebra \eqref{eq:witt-algebra}.
Thus this is a second way of getting an action of the Witt algebra on $\vertex$.
Fortunately it actually coincides with the first way, because the brackets \eqref{eq:virasoro-affine-bracket}
agree with \eqref{eq:witt-action-on-affine}.


\subsection{Consequences of Virasoro}

Moreover, once we realize the action of the Witt algebra ``internally'' via the element $\omega \in \vertex$,
it automatically acts not only on $\vertex$ but on all $\vertex$-modules $\vrep$.
Now we should ask whether it integrates from $\fG$ to the group $\cG$ of coordinate changes.
The answer  depends on $\vrep$.

The best possible situation arises if $L_0$ acts diagonalizably on $\vrep$,
with all eigenvalues integers and bounded below. 
In that case $L_0$ provides a grading on $\vrep$. Moreover
$\fG_+$ acts locally nilpotently\footnote{\ti{Locally nilpotently} means that for every $v \in \fG_+$ and $\chi \in \vrep$ there exists $N$ such that $v^N \chi = 0$.} and $\fG_0$
acts diagonally respecting the relation $\exp(2 \pi \I L_0) = 1$.
Thus both indeed integrate to $\rho: \cG \to \End \vrep$.

The next best situation arises if $\vrep$ is still graded by $L_0$ but 
the eigenvalues are not necessarily integers. Then at least $\fG_+$ integrates
to $\rho: \cG_+ \to \End \vrep$.

An element $\chi \in \vrep$ which is annihilated by all positive modes $L_n$, and obeys $L_0 \chi = h \chi$,
is called a \ti{primary field} of \ti{conformal weight} $h$. 
For example, the elements $J^X \in \vertex$ are primary fields of conformal weight $1$.
The transformation law of primary fields 
under changes of coordinate is relatively simple: if $g(z) = a_1 z + a_2 z^2 + \cdots$ then
$\rho(g) \chi = (a_1)^h \chi$ (note this is well defined only when $h \in \bbZ$, as we discussed in the last
paragraph; we can try to make sense
of it for other $h$, but we will need some extra structure, e.g. a spin structure for $h = \frac12$). 
Said otherwise, $\chi$ ``transforms as an $h$-differential.''
So if $h=1$ then $\chi$ transforms as a $1$-form (we have already used this 
for $\chi = J^X$), if $h=2$ then $\chi$ transforms as a quadratic differential, and so on.

One other interesting case is the Virasoro field $\omega = L_{-2} \vac$ 
itself. This one has $L_0 \omega = 2 \omega$, so you might think it would transform as a 
quadratic differential, but it is not primary for $c \neq 0$: 
indeed $L_2 \omega = [L_2, L_{-2}] \vac = \frac{c}{2} \vac$.
Thus its transformation law is more interesting: under a change of coordinates $g(z) = a_1 z + a_2 z^2 + \cdots$ we have
\begin{align} \label{eq:stress-tensor-transformation}
  \rho(g) \omega &=  a_1^2\,\omega + \frac{c}{2}\,\frac{a_1a_3-a_2^2}{a_1^2} \vac \\
 &= \left( (\de g/\de z)^2 \omega + \frac{c}{12} \{g,z\} \vac \right) \vert_{z=0} \, .
\end{align}
where $\{\cdot,\cdot\}$ denotes the Schwarzian derivative:
$\{g(z),z\} = \frac{g'''(z)}{g'(z)} - \frac32 \left( \frac{g''(z)}{g'(z)} \right)^2$.

\begin{exercise}
Prove \eqref{eq:stress-tensor-transformation}, by (in an appropriate sense) integrating the Virasoro algebra.
\end{exercise}

The Schwarzian derivative is a sort of unfamiliar object to many of us. Nevertheless it appears here
so we have to grapple with it a little. One useful fact about it is that $\{g(z), z\} = 0$ if and only if $g$
is a M\"obius transformation. Here is another way to think about this: 
the group $\PSL(2,\bbC)$ of M\"obius transformations acting on $\bbC \bbP^1$ is
generated by $\ell_{-1} = - \partial_z$, $\ell_0 = -z \partial_z$, $\ell_1 = -z^2 \partial_z$.
(Indeed these three span the space of holomorphic vector fields on $\bbC \bbP^1$.)
Thus, for a M\"obius transformation $g(z)$, the computation of $\rho(g) \omega$ cannot involve
the central charge $c$ --- that can only appear if we use $\ell_2$. This matches the fact that
for a M\"obius transformation we have $\{g(z), z\} = 0$.


\subsection{Moving the marked points}

So far we have only used the $L_n$ with $n \ge 0$. Another 
important feature is that $L_{-1}$ acting on $\vertex$ 
generates translations:
\begin{equation}
  L_{-1} = T \, .
\end{equation}
(To prove this it's enough to use the fact $[L_{-1}, J^X_{(m)}] = -m J^X_{(m-1)}$.)
This means in particular that chiral correlation functions obey the relation
\begin{equation}
  \partial_z \IP{ a(z) \cdots } = \IP{ (L_{-1} a)(z) \cdots }
\end{equation}
One would like to integrate this statement, i.e. to write:
\begin{equation}
  \IP{ a(z+w) \cdots } = \IP{ (\e^{w L_{-1}} a)(z) \cdots }
\end{equation}
This equation is indeed true, in the sense that the 
expansion of the right side in powers of $w$ converges to the left side, so long 
as $\abs{w}$ is smaller than the distance from $z$ to the nearest other insertion.

What is more interesting is that we can do the same thing with $\vertex$-modules inserted, i.e.
for $\chi \in \vrep$ we can have the equation
\begin{equation}
  \partial_z \IP{ \chi(z) \cdots } = \IP{ (L_{-1} \chi)(z) \cdots }
\end{equation}
To formulate this precisely we work in the language of conformal blocks rather than chiral correlation 
systems. Fix a base curve $C$ with punctures and modules, 
and let one puncture $p$ vary while the others $p_i$ are held fixed. 
Then the spaces $\vrep_p \otimes \bigotimes_i (\vrep_i)_{p_i}$
are the fibers of an ind-vector bundle $\cB$ over $C$,
with a subsheaf $\cB_K$ given by the spaces $K(C_p)$ from \eqref{eq:K-subspace}.

Now, first suppose we fix a local coordinate $z$ around $p$.
Then:
\begin{prop} \label{prop:translation-preserves-coinvariants}
If $s$ is a section of $\cB_K$, then $(\partial_z + L_{-1}) s$ is also a section of $\cB_K$.
(Here $L_{-1}$ acts in the tensor factor $\vrep_p$ of $\cB$.)
\end{prop}

Here is the key calculation.
Given an operator $o(a)$ where $a$ is a section of the vertex algebra bundle, we
need to determine $\partial_z o(a)$. That amounts to understanding how the local coefficients
$a_m$ in the expansion of $a$ around $z$ vary as we shift $z$. \fixme{...} This gives
\begin{equation}
  [\partial_z + L_{-1}, o(a)] = 0 \, .
\end{equation}

\begin{example}
Suppose $\vertex = \Heis$ and
$C = \bbC \bbP^1$ with marked points at $w,\infty$.
Then consider the global section $z J$ of the vertex algebra bundle, with
(using $z = (z-w) + w = (z^{-1})^{-1}$)
\begin{equation}
 o(z J) = (J_1 + w J_0) \otimes \bid + \bid \otimes J_{-1} \, .
\end{equation}
Then using $[L_{-1}, J_1] = -J_0$ and $[L_{-1}, J_0] = 0$ we see that
\begin{equation}
  [\partial_w + L_{-1} \otimes \bid, o(z J)] = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
% \begin{multline}
% % [\partial_w + L_{-1} \otimes \bid, o(z J)] = 0
%   (\partial_w + (L_{-1} \otimes 1)) \left( (J_1 + w J_0) \chi_1 \otimes \chi_2 + \chi_1 \otimes J_{-1} \chi_2 \right) = \\
%   (J_1 + w J_0) L_{-1} \chi_1 \otimes \chi_2 + L_{-1} \chi_1 \otimes J_{-1} \chi_2 \ \in \ \cB_K
% \end{multline}
\end{example}

Because of \autoref{prop:translation-preserves-coinvariants} 
it is sensible to ask for a family $\Phi_z$ of conformal blocks obeying
\begin{equation} \label{eq:flat-conformal-blocks}
  \partial_z (\Phi_z(\chi(z), \cdots)) = \Phi_z((\partial_z + L_{-1}) \chi(z), \cdots)
\end{equation}
(The point is that, if $\chi(z)$ is a section of $\cB_K$, then the left side is zero for all $z$; so the right 
side had better also be zero, and it is, thanks to \autoref{prop:translation-preserves-coinvariants}.)
This can also be written in the language of $\cD$-modules:
the operator $\partial_z + L_{-1}$ makes $\cB$ and $\cB_K$ into $\cD$-modules,
and the sheaf of solutions $\Phi$ of \eqref{eq:flat-conformal-blocks} is
$\Hom_{\cD}(\cB / \cB_K, \cO)$.

Note \eqref{eq:flat-conformal-blocks} is equivalent to
\begin{equation} \label{eq:connection-conformal-blocks}
  \partial_z \Phi_z(\chi, \cdots) - \Phi_z(L_{-1} \chi, \cdots) = 0
\end{equation}
which might be written more briefly as $(\partial_z - L_{-1}) \Phi_z = 0$.
So there is a tricky minus sign here, depending whether we look at the
connection on coinvariants or on conformal blocks.

In the form \eqref{eq:connection-conformal-blocks}, our condition looks like it is describing
covariantly constant sections of a connection in the space of conformal blocks.
One has to be a little cautious about what this means, because the space of conformal blocks as we defined it
(pointwise dual of coinvariants) does not necessarily form a bundle over $C$.
But irrespective of this, at least we can speak meaningfully about the solutions 
of \eqref{eq:connection-conformal-blocks}.
In favorable situations, where $\cB / \cB_K$ is actually a vector bundle of finite rank, a $\cD$-module 
structure really does mean a flat connection, and this is a useful intuitive picture even when it is not
literally true.

I am suppressing that bookkeeping here and writing the corresponding local differential equations directly.}

\end{equation}

\end{exercise}

\begin{example} \label{ex:kz-2-reg-1-irreg}
Suppose $\vertex = V^k(\fg)$.
We take $C = \bbC \bbP^1$, with marked points at $z_1$, $z_2$, and $\infty$.
At $z_1$ and $z_2$ we insert parabolic Verma modules $\vrep(M_1)$ and $\vrep(M_2)$
while at $\infty$ we insert the irregular module $\vrep(u=0)$.
The subspace $M_1 \otimes M_2 \otimes \bbC \vac_u \subset \vrep(M_1) \otimes \vrep(M_2) \otimes \vrep(u=0)$ 
represents $\Coinv(C)$ for every $z_1$, $z_2$.
Now to write the operator $L^{(1)}_{-1}$ concretely we use
the fact
\begin{equation}
  L_{-1} = \frac{1}{k + h^\vee} \sum_{a=1}^{\dim \fg} (J^a_{-1} J^a_0 + J^a_{-2} J^a_1 + \cdots)
\end{equation}
All positive modes annihilate $M_1$, so we only have to deal with $J^a_{-1} J^a_0$.
Now consider the function $f = \frac{1}{z-z_1}$. 
We use its expansions around $z_1, z_2, \infty$ to see that
\begin{equation}
 o(f J^a) = J^a_{-1} \otimes \bid \otimes \bid + \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{\bid \otimes J^a_{n} \otimes \bid}{(z_1 - z_2)^{n+1}} + \sum_{n \ge 0} z_1^n (\bid \otimes \bid \otimes J^a_{n+1})
\end{equation}
Acting on $M_1 \otimes M_2 \otimes \bbC \vac_u$
this reduces to
\begin{equation}
  J^a_{-1} \otimes \bid \otimes \bid + \frac{\bid \otimes J^a_{0} \otimes \bid}{z_1 - z_2}
\end{equation}
Thus the image of this operator lies in $K(C)$,
so modulo $K(C)$
we can replace the action of $J_{-1}$ at $z_1$
with the action of $J_0$ at $z_2$. This gives
\begin{equation}
  L^{(1)}_{-1} = - \frac{1}{k + h^\vee} \sum_{a=1}^{\dim \fg} \frac{J^a_0 \otimes J^a_0 \otimes \bid}{z_1 - z_2}
\end{equation}
Very similarly, if we have $n$ marked points in the plane, the action of $L_{-1}$ in the $i$-th factor is
\begin{equation}
  L^{(i)}_{-1} = - \frac{1}{k + h^\vee} \sum_{j \neq i} \sum_{a=1}^{\dim \fg} \frac{(J^a_0)^{(i)} (J^a_0)^{(j)}}{z_i - z_j}
\end{equation}
Altogether then, we have a PDE in the bundle $\Coinv(C) \simeq \bigotimes_i M_i$
over $\Conf_n(\bbC)$, given by the operators
\begin{equation} \label{eq:kz-kohno}
  \partial_{z_i} - \frac{1}{k + h^\vee} \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{T_{ij}}{z_i - z_j}
\end{equation}
where $T_{ij} = \sum_{a=1}^{\dim \fg} (X^a)^{(i)} (X^a)^{(j)}$.

The monodromy of this PDE gives an action of the pure braid group $P_n = \pi_1(\Conf_n(\bbC))$ 
on $\bigotimes_i V_i$. What is this action concretely?
It is easiest to discuss in the case $n=2$; there it is generated by a single loop.
The monodromy of the equation
\begin{equation}
  \partial_z - \frac{1}{k + h^\vee} \frac{T}{z}
\end{equation}
when $z$ goes around $0$ in the positive direction
is just given by the automorphism 
\begin{equation}
  \exp\left(\frac{2 \pi \I}{k + h^\vee} T\right) \in \End(V_1 \otimes V_2)
\end{equation}
In case $n=3$ it is already more interesting.
The local monodromy around $z_i = z_j$ will be conjugate to the automorphism
$\exp\left(\frac{2 \pi \I}{k + h^\vee} T_{ij}\right)$
as long as we are in the ``non-resonant'' situation, where
no two eigenvalues of
$\frac{T_{ij}}{k + h^\vee}$
differ by an integer.
But there are also the change-of-basis maps between the eigenbases at different loci $z_i = z_j$,
which are hard to write explicitly except for $\fg = \fsl_2$.

There is another source of braid group
representations here, namely the theory of the quantum universal enveloping algebra $U_q(\fg)$,
which provides for every $V,W$ an \ti{R-matrix} $R_{VW} \in \End(V \otimes W)$
obeying on $V_1 \otimes V_2 \otimes V_3$ the relation
\begin{equation}
  R_{12} R_{13} R_{23} = R_{23} R_{13} R_{12} \, ,
\end{equation}
and compatible with morphisms and tensor products (in an appropriate sense, so-called \ti{hexagon relation}).
For example, if $\fg = \fsl_2$ and $V$, $W$ are both the fundamental representation, then $R_{VW}$ is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & q & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1-q^2 & q & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
Given this $R$-matrix and representations $V_1, \dots, V_n$ of $\fg$,
one can construct a representation of the pure braid group $P_n$ on $\bigotimes_i V_i$.
Then we have:
\begin{thm}[Drinfeld-Kohno]
For generic $k$, the representation of $P_n$ on $\otimes_{i=1}^n V_i$ coming from monodromy of coinvariants for $V^k(\fg)$
is equivalent to the one coming from $U_q(\fg)$, with the relation $q = \exp( \frac{\pi \I}{k + h^\vee} )$.
\end{thm}

Note it really says \ti{equivalent} and not \ti{equal}. You couldn't really ask for more here, 
since the representation given by monodromy of coinvariants depends on a choice of basepoint and
generating loops. (But we will say something better in a bit.)

Here ``generic $k$'' is supposed to mean there are no resonances.
(Apparently there are different versions of the statement, e.g. Drinfeld's version is
really an $\hbar$-adic statement with $\hbar = 1 / (k + h^\vee)$).

For a quick check, looking up the $R$-matrices one sees that 
the operator $R_{12} \circ R_{12}$ on $V_1 \otimes V_2$
acts as $q^{2 T_{12}}$,
matching with the monodromy of coinvariants which we discussed above. 
This proves the Drinfeld-Kohno theorem in case $n=2$.

\end{example}

\subsection{Duals}

Given a $\vertex$-module $\vrep$, we can define its \ti{dual} 
in a tautological way: 
we imagine putting $\vrep$ at $0$ on the sphere, and then
the dual consists of all objects which
can be placed at $\infty$.

\begin{defn}[Dual module]
Say $\vertex$ is a conformal vertex algebra, and let $\vrep$ be an ordinary $\vertex$-module.
Then let $\vrep^* = \bigoplus \vrep_n^*$ be the graded dual space,\footnote{Some authors would call this $\vrep'$ and reserve $\vrep^*$ for the vector space dual without regard for grading.} 
and define $Y^{\vrep^*}(a,z) \in \End \vrep^*[[z]]$ by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dual-vertex-operator}
  (Y^{\vrep^*}(a,z) \alpha)  \cdot  \chi = \alpha \cdot (Y^\vrep(\e^{z L_1} (-z^{-2})^{L_0} a, z^{-1}) \chi) \, .
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

This definition looks a little complicated, but it is
codifying something that was implicit in our definition of
conformal blocks for a general vertex algebra.
Indeed, around a point $z \in \bbC^\times$ there are two natural
pointed coordinate systems: $t(w) = w - z$ and $t'(w) = 1/w - 1/z$.
The transformation $g(z) = \e^{z L_1} (-z^{-2})^{L_0}$ appearing in
\eqref{eq:dual-vertex-operator} is the action of the
change of coordinates from $t$ to $t'$.
Then \eqref{eq:dual-vertex-operator} is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dual-vertex-operator-abstract}
  (Y^{\vrep^*}(a,z) \alpha)  \cdot  \chi = \alpha \cdot (Y^\vrep(\rho(g(z)) a, z^{-1}) \chi) \, .
\end{equation}

In the case of $\vertex = V^k(\fg)$ it becomes more familiar:
\begin{example}
If $\vertex = V^k(\fg)$ then \eqref{eq:dual-vertex-operator} implies
\begin{equation}
  (Y^{\vrep^*}(J^X,z) \alpha) \cdot \chi = \alpha \cdot (Y^\vrep(- z^{-2} J^X, z^{-1}) \chi)
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dual-pairing-simplified}
  J^X_n \alpha \cdot \chi + \alpha \cdot J^X_{-n} \chi = 0
\end{equation}
and conversely checking \eqref{eq:dual-pairing-simplified} is
sufficient to verify \eqref{eq:dual-vertex-operator}.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}
Consider the involution $\lambda \mapsto \lambda^* = -w_0(\lambda)$, where $w_0$ is the longest
element in the Weyl group $W(\fg)$. (For $\fg = \fsl_2$ we just have $\lambda^* = \lambda$.)
Let $L_\lambda$ be the finite-dimensional $\fg$-module with highest weight $\lambda$. Then $L_{\lambda^*}$
is the dual of $L_{\lambda}$. Prove similarly that the dual of $\vrep(L_\lambda)$ is $\vrep(L_{\lambda^*})$, provided that $k$ is generic
(so that the invariant bilinear form on $\vrep(L_\lambda)$ is 
nondegenerate).
\end{exercise}

\subsection{Varying the curve}

So far we discussed how to vary conformal blocks when we move marked points, i.e.
we got a connection on the bundle of conformal blocks over $C$.
But the Virasoro action on $\vertex$ actually allows us to do more: we can vary the curve $C$ itself,
thus obtaining (roughly) a connection over the moduli space of curves.

Deformations of $C$ are realized by the cohomology $H^1(C,T)$.
The details of how we describe this connection depends on exactly how we realize the deformation of
$C$. 

First: suppose given a Cech cocycle $v$, i.e. cover $C$ by patches $U_\alpha$ and give
vector fields $v_{\alpha \beta}$ on overlaps $U_{\alpha \beta}$, obeying
$v_{\alpha \beta} + v_{\beta \gamma} + v_{\gamma \alpha} = 0$.
Then the corresponding variation of chiral correlation functions is given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:complex-variation-cech}
  \partial_v \IP{\cdots} = \frac{1}{2 \pi \I} \sum_{\alpha < \beta} \int_{z \in c_{\alpha \beta}} v_{\alpha \beta}(z) \IP{T(z) \cdots}
\end{equation}
The contours $c_{\alpha \beta}$ are defined as follows.
Choose an ordering $<$ arbitrarily on the index set.
Shrink each $U_\alpha$ slightly to $V_\alpha$ with smooth boundary, such that they still cover $C$, and the boundaries meet transversely; then let $c_{\alpha \beta} = \overline{V_\alpha} \cap U_\beta$, with the boundary orientation induced by $V_\alpha$, for each $\alpha < \beta$.
\input{figures/cech-cover-contours}



A simple but useful example arises when we cover $C$ by two open sets $U_{1,2}$, intersecting in an annulus.
Then the deformation is induced by a single vector field $v_{12}$ on the annulus,
and \eqref{eq:complex-variation-cech} becomes an integral over a loop $c_{12}$ around the annulus:
\begin{equation}
  \partial_v \IP{\cdots} = \frac{1}{2 \pi \I} \int_{z \in c} v_{12}(z) \IP{T(z) \cdots}
\end{equation}

Second: suppose given an infinitesimal Beltrami differential $\mu \in H^{0,1}(T)$.
Then
\begin{equation} \label{eq:complex-variation-beltrami}
  \partial_\mu \IP{\cdots} = \frac{1}{2 \pi \I} \int_{z \in C} \mu(z) \IP{T(z) \cdots}
\end{equation}
(Note this is well defined only as long as the support of $\mu(z)$ avoids the points where the insertions $\cdots$ are placed.
This is a good feature, not a bug; it allows us to make canonical sense of what we mean by varying
the curve while ``holding the marked points fixed.'' Every deformation class can be 
represented by a $\mu(z)$ avoiding any particular set of points.)

The formulas \eqref{eq:complex-variation-beltrami} and \eqref{eq:complex-variation-cech} are related essentially
by integration by parts.

There is something to prove here; for notational convenience we consider
only the case of a single marked point, carrying the representation $\vrep$:
\begin{prop}
Fix $v \in H^1(C,T)$. 
Let $\delta_v \in \End (\vrep)$ be the operator induced by
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{2 \pi \I} \sum_{\alpha < \beta} \int_{z \in c_{\alpha \beta}} v_{\alpha \beta}(z) T(z) \,
\end{equation}
and suppose that
$s$ is a section of the bundle $\cB_K$ over the moduli space of
Riemann surfaces with a marked point.
Then $(\partial_v + \delta_v) s$ is also a section of $\cB_K$.
\end{prop}


\subsection{Virasoro twisting}

One caution: the formula \eqref{eq:complex-variation-beltrami} depends on the local coordinate we use. If $T(z)$ transformed as a quadratic
differential, then $\mu(z) T(z)$ would be a $(1,1)$-form, perfect for integration. 
But the truth is that $T(z)$ is not quite a quadratic differential, rather it has
the transformation law \eqref{eq:stress-tensor-transformation}. Using that formula we see that changing the local coordinate $z \to g(z)$ changes
the integrand in \eqref{eq:complex-variation-beltrami} by
\begin{equation}
  \frac{c}{12} \mu(z) \{g(z),z\} \IP{\cdots}
\end{equation}
There are a few ways of thinking about this problem.
One, the most concrete, is to fix \ti{complex projective structures} on our surfaces:
\begin{defn}[Complex projective structure]
A \ti{complex projective structure} on a Riemann surface $C$ is a choice of an atlas of coordinate 
systems for which the transition maps are M\"obius transformations.
\end{defn}
Every surface admits a complex projective structure, e.g. from Fuchsian uniformization; indeed Fuchsian uniformization gives
a complex projective structure where the transition maps are in $PSL(2,\bbR)$, not only $PSL(2,\bbC)$. 
But for our purposes we don't want to use that one: we want to use one which varies holomorphically as we vary $C$
(i.e. there should exist coordinate systems in the atlas which vary holomorphically as functions of the moduli of $C$).
% One which would be OK is the \ti{Bergman} projective structure \fixme{...}

Given two complex projective structures we can consider their \ti{difference}:
\begin{defn}[Difference of complex projective structures]
Let $S_1$ and $S_2$ be two complex projective structures on $C$.
There is a globally defined holomorphic quadratic differential $S_2 - S_1$ 
on $C$, given locally by $S_2 - S_1 = \{z,w\} \, \de z^2$, where
$z$ is a local coordinate in $S_1$ and $w$ is a coordinate in $S_2$.  
\end{defn}
For any smooth compact Riemann surface $C$, the complex projective structures on $C$ form a torsor over the space $H^0(C, K^2)$ of holomorphic
quadratic differentials. Thus complex projective structures make up a holomorphic affine bundle over the moduli space of curves.

At any rate, once we have locally fixed a holomorphic section of the bundle of 
complex projective structures, \eqref{eq:complex-variation-beltrami} 
defines an honest connection locally over the moduli of curves.

\subsection{Plumbing conformal blocks}

\input{figures/plumbing-annuli-identification}
\begin{defn}[Plumbing Riemann surfaces]
Suppose $C_1$ and $C_2$ are two Riemann surfaces with marked points $p_1$, $p_2$. Fix holomorphic coordinates $z_1$, $z_2$ around these points. Also fix parameters $r_1, r_2 > 0$ such that the $z_i$-coordinate patch contains $\{ \abs{z_i} < r_i \}$, 
and fix $q \in \bbC^\times$ with $0 < \abs{q} < r_1 r_2$.
Let
\begin{equation}
  C'_1 = C_1 \setminus \{\abs{z_1} \le \abs{q} / r_2\}, \qquad  
  C'_2 = C_2 \setminus \{\abs{z_2} \le \abs{q} / r_1\}  \, .
\end{equation}
Then we have annuli 
\begin{equation}
 A_1 = \{ \abs{q} / r_2 < \abs{z_1} < r_1  \} \subset C'_1, \qquad A_2 = \{ \abs{q} / r_1 < \abs{z_2} < r_2  \} \subset C'_2 \, .
\end{equation}
Finally let $C_q = (C'_1 \sqcup C'_2) / \sim$,
where $\sim$ is the relation
\begin{equation}
  z_1 \sim q / z_2, \qquad z_1 \in A_1, \quad z_2 \in A_2 \, .
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

% It creates a cylinder of modulus $\frac{1}{2\pi} \log \frac{r_1 r_2}{\abs{q}}$.
\begin{exercise}
Prove that the plumbed surface 
$C_q$ does not depend on the choice of the $r_i$,
and that it depends on the local coordinates only through their $1$-jets at the marked points; rescaling the local coordinate on
either side rescales $q$. Said more abstractly, we use a bilinear pairing $b$ between the tangent spaces at the 
marked points; then identifying each side locally with its tangent space, the plumbed surface is $b(z_1,z_2) = q$.
\end{exercise}

One can also think of plumbing as connecting the two sides
$C''_i = C_i \setminus \{\abs{z_i} \le r_i \}$ by adding in 
a cylinder, whose modulus is $\abs{q} / r_1 r_2$.
Conformally, this cylinder is equivalent to a tube with circumference 
$2 \pi$ and length $\log (r_1 r_2 / \abs{q})$. In the limit $q \to 0$ this cylinder becomes infinitely long, and the plumbed surface develops
a node.

\input{figures/plumbing-cross-ratio}
\begin{exercise}
Suppose that $C_1$ and $C_2$ are spheres, with the
standard coordinates $z_1$ and $z_2$,
each with marked points at $0,1,\infty$. Fix some $q$ 
with $\abs{q} < 1$. Plumbing together the 
marked points at $z_1=0$ and $z_2=0$,
the plumbed surface $C_q$ is again a sphere, with
four marked points. Prove that the invariant cross-ratio 
of these four points is $q$.
\end{exercise}

Now we claim it's possible to make conformal blocks on the plumbed surface from conformal blocks on the original two,
as follows. 

Suppose given conformal blocks $\Phi_1$ on $C_1$ and $\Phi_2$ on $C_2$, with $\vrep$, $\vrep^*$ inserted at
marked points $p_1$, $p_2$, and possibly 
other insertions $\chi_1$ on $C_1$, $\chi_2$ on $C_2$.
Then let $h$ be the minimum conformal weight in $\vrep$, and define
\begin{equation} \label{eq:block-gluing}
  \Phi(\chi_1, \chi_2) = \sum_{n \ge h} q^{n} \sum_i \Phi_1(\chi_1, \xi_{i,n}) \Phi_2(\xi^*_{i,n}, \chi_2)
\end{equation}
where $(\xi_{i,n})$ and $(\xi^*_{i,n})$ are dual bases for $\vrep_n$ and $\vrep^*_n$, with respect to the invariant pairing.\footnote{Or, without choosing a basis, 
one could say we insert the operator $q^{L_0}$ into
the $\vrep \otimes \vrep^*$ slots of $\Phi_1 \otimes \Phi_2$.}
Note that in the limit $q \to 0$ the leading term comes from the 
primaries of weight $h$ in $\vrep$ contribute, and if we rescale by an overall factor $q^{-h}$ then these
are the only ones that survive the limit; for finite $q$ the whole
tower of descendants is involved.

The formula \eqref{eq:block-gluing} makes sense for $q \in \bbR_{+}$.
If the conformal weights $n \notin \bbZ$, then 
it is multivalued as a function of $q \in \bbC$. 
To make it well defined we can 
replace $q \to \exp(2 \pi \I \tau)$;
then we get a single-valued function of $\tau$.

Here is the idea of the definition \eqref{eq:block-gluing}. 
Suppose we consider a completely arbitrary linear functional of $\chi_1$
and $\chi_2$. We could try to build chiral correlation functions out of such a functional, and they will make
sense at least on formal discs around the insertions. 
If our functional is of the form $\Phi_1(\chi_1, \xi) \Phi_2(\xi', \chi_2)$ then the chiral
correlation functions will make sense (obeying all the usual properties) 
on $C_1 \cup C_2$ with the punctures $p_1$, $p_2$ deleted.
More generally the same is true if we take any $\sum_j \Phi_1(\chi_1, \xi_j) \Phi_2(\xi'_j, \chi_2)$.
Now, what we want to do is to engineer the object $\cI = \sum_j \xi_j \otimes \xi'_j$
in such a way that the chiral correlation
functions we obtain on the annuli $A_1$ and $A_2$ are the same, and thus can be glued to give chiral
correlation functions on $C$. 
This will happen if we choose
\begin{equation}
  \cI = \sum_{n \ge h} q^{n} \sum_i \xi_{i,n} \otimes \xi^*_{i,n} = \sum_{n \ge h} \sum_i (q^{L_0} \xi_{i,n}) \otimes \xi^*_{i,n} \, .
\end{equation}
Indeed, suppose we insert $a(z_1)$ on $C_1$.
Expanding near $z_1 = 0$ we see that inside the correlation function this replaces $\cI$ by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gluing-check-1}
 \sum_{n,i} (Y^\vrep (a, z_1) q^{L_0} \xi_{i,n}) \otimes \xi^*_{i,n} \, .
\end{equation}
On the other hand, if we insert $a(z_2)$ near $z_2 = 0$ on $C_2$ we get
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{n,i} q^{L_0} \xi_{i,n} \otimes (Y^{\vrep^*} (a, z_2) \xi^*_{i,n}) \, .
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{eq:dual-vertex-operator-abstract}, this is
\begin{equation}
\sum_{n,i} q^{L_0} (Y^{\vrep}(\rho(g(z_2)) a, z_2^{-1}) \xi_{i,n}) \otimes \xi^*_{i,n}
\end{equation}
with $g(z_2)$ the pointed version of the transformation $z \to 1/z$.
Commuting the $q^{L_0}$ through we get
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gluing-check-2}
\sum_{n,i} (Y^{\vrep}(\rho(g(z_2,q)) a, q z_2^{-1}) q^{L_0} \xi_{i,n}) \otimes \xi^*_{i,n}
\end{equation}
where $g(z_2,q)$ is the pointed version of $z \to q/z$.
Finally, using the coordinate covariance of $Y^\vrep$ and $z_1 = q / z_2$ we see that
\eqref{eq:gluing-check-1} and \eqref{eq:gluing-check-2} match, as desired.

It is not at all obvious that the sum \eqref{eq:block-gluing} converges, but at least \ti{formally} it gives a conformal block:
\begin{exercise}
Assuming that the sum in \eqref{eq:block-gluing} converges, prove that it gives a conformal block on the
plumbed surface $C$.
\end{exercise}

More generally, instead of having two surfaces we can just suppose
$C_0$ is a surface (either disconnected or connected),
with two punctures carrying $\vrep$ and $\vrep^*$, and a conformal block;

\subsection{Unitarity}

So far all the structures we have discussed were complex-linear.
But in addition, we may have another symmetry, which introduces
some useful \ti{positivity} in the story.
In this section we let $\overline{V}$ denote the complex conjugate
vector space to $V$.

Suppose $\vertex$ is a conformal vertex algebra, and
equip $\vertex$ with a map $\sigma: \vertex \to \overline{\vertex}$,
such that $\sigma^2 = \bid$ (sometimes called PCT symmetry).
For instance, if $\vertex = V^k(\fg)$ one way to get such a $\sigma$
would be to choose a real structure on $\fg$.

Then we can define:
\begin{defn}[Conjugate $\vertex$-module]
Suppose $\vrep$ is an ordinary $\vertex$-module.
Then we make $\overline{\vrep}$ into a $\vertex$-module by
the action
\begin{equation}
  Y^{\overline{\vrep}}(a,z) \overline{\chi} = \overline{Y^\vrep(\sigma(a),\overline{z}) \chi} \, .
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}[Hermitian pairing]
A \ti{Hermitian pairing} on $\vrep$ is an isomorphism $\overline{\vrep} \simeq \vrep^*$, where $\vrep^*$ is the dual module defined above.
Such a pairing induces a Hermitian pairing $(\cdot,\cdot)$ on 
the vector space $\vrep$.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}[Unitarity]
We call a $\vertex$-module $\vrep$ \ti{unitary} if it is
equipped with a positive definite Hermitian pairing.
\end{defn}

The Heisenberg vertex algebra can be made unitary,
but as far as I understand this is not true for
more general $V^k(\fg)$ or for Virasoro.
But we will discuss some more interesting 
unitary vertex algebras below.

\section{The rational case}

\subsection{Category of ordinary modules}

So far we have been working with the vertex algebra $\vertex^k(\fg)$ at generic $k$.
Now let us instead take $k \in \bbN$ and 
consider the simple quotient $\vertex = L_k(\fg)$.
In this case some things become simpler.
In particular, the category of modules becomes simpler.

\begin{defn}
An \ti{ordinary $\vertex$-module} is a $\vertex$-module for which 
$L_0$ acts diagonalizably, with eigenvalues bounded below, and
finite-dimensional eigenspaces.
\end{defn}

Let $\cC$ be the category of ordinary modules for $L_k(\fg)$.


\subsection{The premodular tensor category structure}

We will explain in this section that $\cC$ has a bunch of nice 
structures, which together make it a \ti{premodular tensor
category}.


\subsubsection{Semisimplicity}

\begin{prop}[Nice properties of ordinary modules for $L_k(\fg)$] For $k \in \bbN$, every ordinary module for $L_k(\fg)$ is a finite direct sum of simple ones. (``Simple'' means they have no 
proper submodules.)
The simple ones are irreducible quotients of parabolic Verma modules, with highest weights
$\lambda$ subject to the condition $\lambda \cdot \theta^\vee \le k$, where $\theta^\vee \in \fh$
is the highest coroot of $\fg$. Call these simple modules $\vrep(\lambda)$.
\end{prop}

In particular: $\cC$ is a $\bbC$-linear semisimple abelian category,
with finitely many simple objects.
This fact already has lots of consequences which we will use repeatedly.
For instance, every simple module has
$\Hom(\cS_i,\cS_i) = \bbC$, and every 
module $\vrep$ has a canonical decomposition
isomorphism
\begin{equation}
  \vrep \to \bigoplus_i V_\vrep^i \otimes \cS_i
\end{equation}
where the $V_\vrep^i$ are finite-dimensional vector spaces
(given by $V_\vrep^i = \Hom(\cS_i, \vrep)$.)

% The proof of complete reducibility uses the fact that every ordinary module for $L_k(\fg)$ carries 
% a positive definite Hermitian form.

Concretely the condition $\lambda \cdot \theta^\vee \le k$ says that all the eigenvalues of $\theta^\vee \in \fh$
acting on $M_\lambda$ are $\le k$.
To explain why we need this condition, recall that
$(E_{-\theta})_{-1}^{k+1} \vac = 0$ in $L_k(\fg)$. Acting on the primary part $M_\lambda \subset \vrep(\lambda)$,
the zero mode of $(E_{-\theta})_{-1}^{k+1} \vac$ acts by $(E_{-\theta})_{0}^{k+1}$. It follows that
$(E_{-\theta})_{0}^{k+1}$ must act by zero on $M_\lambda$. 
But now consider the $\fsl_2$ spanned by
\begin{equation}
  E = E_\theta, \quad F = E_{-\theta}, \quad H = [E_\theta, E_{-\theta}] = \theta^\vee
\end{equation}
(For $\fg = \fsl_N$ we would have $E_{\theta} = E_{1N}$ and $E_{-\theta} = E_{N1}$, i.e., 
the $\fsl_2$ we are talking about is just sitting at the outermost corners of our $\fsl_N$ matrices.)
Then the largest $H$-eigenvalue occurring in $M_\lambda$ is $\lambda \cdot \theta^\vee$,
and using $\fsl_2$ representation theory we get that
$F^{k+1} = 0$ on $M_\lambda$ iff $\lambda \cdot \theta^\vee \le k$.

\begin{example}
If $\fg = \fsl_2$ the root lattice is spanned by a single root $\alpha$. The coroot $\theta^\vee = \alpha^\vee = H$ has 
$\theta^\vee \cdot \alpha = 2$.
The weight lattice is spanned by $\omega = \frac12 \alpha$.
A dominant weight $\lambda = n \omega$ thus has $\lambda \cdot \theta^\vee = n$.
So the condition $\lambda \cdot \theta^\vee \le k$ restricts us to the $k+1$ possible highest weights
\begin{equation}
  \lambda \in \{0, \omega, \dots, k \omega\}
\end{equation}
corresponding to the irreducible $\fsl_2$-representations of 
dimensions $1, 2, \dots, k+1$.
\end{example}



\subsubsection{Tensor products}

What happens when we have two $\vertex$-modules $\vrep_1$, $\vrep_2$
and bring them close together?

Optimistically we might hope that there is a notion of tensor product
of $\vertex$-modules, with
\begin{equation} \label{eq:boxtimes-coeff}
  \cS_i \boxtimes \cS_j \simeq \bigoplus_k N_{ij}^k \cS_k
\end{equation}
where the \ti{fusion coefficient}
$N_{ij}^k \in \bbZ_{\ge 0}$ is 
the dimension of the space of conformal blocks on $\bbC \bbP^1$
with $\cS_i, \cS_j, \cS_k^*$ inserted. (Note this dimension is independent
of \ti{where} the three points are.)

To start thinking about this, consider:
\begin{example}
Suppose $\fg = \fsl_2$.
Then we have simple modules $\cS_n$ corresponding to weights $n \omega$.
Consider a conformal block $\Phi$ on $\bbC \bbP^1$
with modules $\cS_a$, $\cS_b$, $\cS_c$ inserted. We already know from our discussion of 
$\vertex^k(\fg)$-blocks that $\Phi$ is determined by an intertwiner
$\Phi_0 \in \Hom_\fg(M_a \otimes M_b \otimes M_c, \triv)$.
Recalling that $M_n = M_n^*$, such an intertwiner exists 
whenever $M_c$ appears in $M_a \otimes M_b$, i.e. 
whenever $a+b+c \in 2\bbZ$ and 
$\abs{a-b} \le c \le a+b$.
But since we consider $L_k(\fg)$ instead of $V^k(\fg)$
we have an additional constraint now, 
coming from $(E_\theta)_{-1}^{k+1} \vac = 0$. 
This constraint turns out to impose the condition that $a + b + c \le 2k$,
known as the \ti{depth rule}, first derived in \cite{GepnerWitten1986}.
\end{example}

This answer is a little weird. It means in particular that $\vrep(M_a) \boxtimes \vrep(M_b)$ cannot be $\vrep(M_a \otimes M_b)$.
\begin{example}
Suppose $\fg = \fsl_2$ and $k=3$.
Then we have simple modules $\cS_0, \dots, \cS_3$ and for instance this rule would say
\begin{equation}
  \cS_2 \boxtimes \cS_2 \simeq \cS_0 \oplus \cS_2, \qquad \cS_3 \boxtimes \cS_3 \simeq \cS_0 \, .
\end{equation}
(Note that in $\cS_3 \boxtimes \cS_3$ the object $\cS_2$ does not appear, even though it would have 
appeared in the naive tensor product, and it is an allowed object of the category!)
\end{example}

We can define the \ti{fusion semiring} as 
the set of equivalence-classes of objects 
of $\cC$, with addition given by $\oplus$ and product $\boxtimes$.
It is commutative and associative (you could check this by hand
in any particular case, or we will explain it below).
By formally adjoining negatives 
of objects (Grothendieck ring) we get the \ti{fusion ring}.
\begin{exercise}
Suppose $\fg = \fsl_2$ and $k=3$. Check directly that the fusion ring
is associative, and prove that it is isomorphic to $\bbZ[x] / (x^4 - 3x^2 + 1)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{example}
More generally, if $\fg = \fsl_2$, the fusion ring is 
$\bbZ[x] / U_{k+1}(\frac{x}{2})$ where $U_{k+1}$ is a Chebyshev polynomial.
(It represents the class of the $(k+1)$-dimensional irreducible
representation; so we get the fusion ring by starting with the 
ring of finite-dimensional $\fsl_2$-modules and then formally
``setting that representation to zero.'')
\end{example}

We could try to produce a tensor product ``by hand'' by just
defining $\cS_i \boxtimes \cS_j = \bigoplus_{k} \bbC^{N_{ij}^k} \otimes \cS_k$, or more generally
$\vrep_1 \boxtimes \vrep_2 = \bigoplus_k V^{\vrep_1}_i \otimes V^{\vrep_2}_j \otimes \bbC^{N_{ij}^k} \otimes \cS_k$.
This won't have the properties we usually want.
For example, it isn't associative in any reasonable way,
i.e. we don't have any canonical isomorphism
$(\cS_i \boxtimes \cS_j) \boxtimes \cS_k \simeq \cS_i \boxtimes (\cS_j \boxtimes \cS_k)$. (It is true that the two sides decompose isomorphically into simple objects, but we don't have
a map between them; it would mean giving a map
$\bigoplus_\ell \bbC^{N_{ij}^\ell} \otimes \bbC^{N_{\ell k}^m} \simeq \bigoplus_\ell \bbC^{N_{jk}^\ell} \otimes \bbC^{N_{\ell i}^m}$.)

% It is not even functorial in any obvious way, i.e. we don't know how to take tensor
% products of general representations, only ones which come to us already as direct sums of the
% simple objects $\cS_i$.

Nevertheless, for any modules $\vrep_1$, $\vrep_2$ we \ti{can} define a product module 
$\vrep_1 \boxtimes \vrep_2$, for which we do have \eqref{eq:boxtimes-coeff}.
More canonically 
we have a family of modules $\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2$ 
parameterized by $z_1, z_2 \in \bbC^2 \setminus \{z_1 = z_2\}$,
with the property that
$\Hom(\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2, W)$
is a space of 3-point conformal blocks with
$\vrep_1, \vrep_2, W$ at $z_1, z_2, \infty$ respectively.
The modules $\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2$ are all isomorphic (they have the same decomposition into simple objects), 
but not canonically so.
Then we define $\vrep_1 \boxtimes \vrep_2$ by fixing (arbitrarily) $z_1 = 0$, $z_2 = 1$.

The module
$\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2$ is not $\vrep_1 \otimes \vrep_2$ as a vector space.
Nevertheless, informally we should think of $\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2$ as follows: it is some kind of completion of $\vrep_1 \otimes \vrep_2$,
made into a $\vertex$-module by the rule that any mode $a_{(n)}$ acts
by a big contour-integral encircling $0$, $z_1$ and $z_2$,
which by contour-deformation we can replace with small contours 
encircling $z_1$ and $z_2$ alone.

Now we can formulate the existence of tensor products, for
finite enough vertex algebras:

\begin{defn}[Rational and strongly rational vertex algebras] \ 
\begin{itemize}
\item We say $\vertex$ is \ti{rational} if
there are finitely many simple ordinary modules,
each ordinary module is a direct sum of simple modules,
and all $N_{ij}^k$ are finite.\footnote{Warning: there seem to be slight variants of this definition in the literature.}

\item Define the \ti{$C_2$ subspace} of $\vertex$ by
\begin{equation}
  C_2(\vertex) = \{ a_{(-2)} b \mid a, b \in \vertex \} \, .
\end{equation}
We say $\vertex$ is \ti{strongly rational} if $\vertex$ is rational,
$\vertex \simeq \vertex^*$, and $\dim \vertex / C_2(\vertex) < \infty$.\footnote{By the way, the space $\vertex / C_2(\vertex)$ is naturally a Poisson algebra, with product $a_{(-1)} b$ and bracket $a_{(0)} b$. It's an interesting object; its spectrum is called the \ti{associated variety} of $\vertex$. Right now though we are talking about the situation where 
$\vertex / C_2(\vertex)$ is finite-dimensional, so its
spectrum is zero-dimensional. In this case $\vertex$ is also called
\ti{$C_2$-cofinite} or \ti{lisse}.}
\end{itemize}
\end{defn}

In particular $\vertex = L_k(\fg)$ is strongly rational, for $k \in \bbN$.

\begin{prop}[Existence of tensor products]
Suppose $\vertex$ is rational and $\vrep_1$, $\vrep_2$ are ordinary modules.
Then there is a $\vertex$-module $\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2$
with a $(z_1,z_2)$-intertwining map
\begin{equation}
 \vrep_1 \otimes \vrep_2 \to \overline{\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2} 
\end{equation}
such that any other $(z_1,z_2)$-intertwining 
map $\vrep_1 \otimes \vrep_2 \to \overline{W}$
factors uniquely through this one, via a map of $\vertex$-modules,
$\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2 \to W$.
\end{prop}

See \cite{HuangLepowsky1995TensorI} for this definition, \cite{HuangLepowsky1995TensorIII} for the construction.
(It's unpleasantly technical, but the idea is to realize $\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2$ through how it behaves in correlation functions,
i.e. define an element $\eta \in \vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2$ as a linear map on a certain large space of
``possible insertions outside the disc.'')

$\Hom(\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2, W)$
can be identified as the space of conformal blocks with insertions
of $\vrep_1, \vrep_2, W^*$ at $z_1, z_2, \infty$.
Another is that $\vrep \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vertex \simeq \vrep$ canonically.

\subsubsection{Braiding}

For the usual tensor product of vector spaces we have a canonical isomorphism
$V_1 \otimes V_2 \simeq V_2 \otimes V_1$, and repeating it twice gives the identity
on $V_1 \otimes V_2$.

Here the situation is different: we don't have any simple reason to expect
$\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2 \simeq \vrep_2 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_1$.
What we have instead for free is
\begin{equation}
  \vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2 \simeq \vrep_2 \boxtimes_{z_2,z_1} \vrep_1 \, .
\end{equation}
Still, for any $W$, using the operator $\partial_z + L_{-1}$ we can define an isomorphism
\begin{equation}
  \Hom(\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2, W) \to \Hom(\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z'_1,z'_2} \vrep_2, W)
\end{equation}
given any path from $(z_1,z_2)$ to $(z'_1, z'_2)$ which avoids the diagonal.
In particular, this gives two \ti{braiding} isomorphisms (depending which way $z_1$ and $z_2$ exchange),
\begin{equation}
  b_{12}^\pm(W): \Hom(\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2, W) \to \Hom(\vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2, W) \, ,
\end{equation}
obeying $b_{12}^+ b_{21}^- = \bid$.

Finally, knowing this map for every $W$ is enough to define 
\input{figures/braiding-overcrossing}
\begin{equation}
 b_{12}^\pm:  \vrep_1 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_2 \to \vrep_2 \boxtimes_{z_1,z_2} \vrep_1 \, .
\end{equation}
and in particular
\begin{equation}
 b_{12}^\pm:  \vrep_1 \boxtimes \vrep_2 \to \vrep_2 \boxtimes \vrep_1 \, .
\end{equation}

Sometimes we just use $b$ for $b^+$.

Concretely, what is the data of $b$?
Use the canonical decomposition 
$\cS_i \boxtimes \cS_j = \bigoplus_k V_{ij}^k \otimes \cS_k$ (so
$V_{ij}^k = V^k_{\cS_i \boxtimes \cS_j}$); then
$b$ amounts to a linear isomorphism
\begin{equation}
  R_{ij}^k: V_{ij}^k \to V_{ji}^k \, .
\end{equation}
It becomes a concrete matrix if we choose bases in all $V_{ij}^k$.

\subsubsection{Factorization}

Now we need another important analytic fact:

\begin{prop}[Factorization for strongly rational vertex algebras] \label{prop:factorization-strongly-rational}
Suppose $\vertex$ is strongly rational. Then
every conformal block on the sphere
with 4 ordinary modules inserted can be obtained by plumbing
two blocks with 3 ordinary modules inserted, giving 
when $z_1$ is close to $z_2$ and $z_3$ is close to $z_4$
\fixme{draw a picture here}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:factorization-four-punctures}
  \Conf(\vrep_1, \vrep_2, \vrep_3, \vrep_4) = \bigoplus_i \Conf(\vrep_1, \vrep_2, \cS_i) \otimes \Conf(\cS^*_i, \vrep_3, \vrep_4) \, .
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

As a practical matter, part of what this means is that we can describe the
behavior of correlation functions when two primary insertions 
$\vrep_1(z_1)$ and $\vrep_2(z_2)$ are brought close together
using the $L_{-1}$ action. We use the following:
\begin{exercise}
Let $\vertex$ be any conformal vertex algebra.
Consider a conformal block on the sphere
with ordinary module insertions $\vrep_{1,2,3}$ at $0,w,\infty$,
with primary vectors $\chi_{1,2,3}$ of conformal weights $h_i$.
Prove that the differential equation
\begin{equation}
  \partial_w \IP{\chi_1(0) \chi_2(w) \chi_3(\infty)} = \IP{\chi_1(0) L_{-1} \chi_2(w) \chi_3(\infty)}
\end{equation}
implies that 
\begin{equation}
  \IP{\chi_1(0) \chi_2(w) \chi_3(\infty)} = \alpha w^{-h_1-h_2+h_3}
\end{equation}
for some $\alpha(\chi_1,\chi_2,\chi_3) \in \bbC$. (Hint: use the coinvariant constraints to 
relate the action of $L_{-1}$ at $w$ to the action of $L_{\ge 0}$
at all three marked points; then you will get a closed equation for
$\IP{\chi_1(0) \chi_2(w) \chi_3(\infty)}$, which can be solved directly.)
\end{exercise}

Plugging this into the plumbing we see that for 
primary states $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$, any conformal block in the summand (``channel'') labeled by
$\cS_i$ in \eqref{eq:factorization-four-punctures} 
will obey (inside correlation functions)
\begin{equation}
  \chi_1(z_1) \chi_2(z_2) = (z_1 - z_2)^{h_i - h_2 - h_1} \chi_i(z_2)  + \cdots
\end{equation}
for some primary $\chi_i$ in $\cS_i$,
and in particular the monodromy when $z_1$ is taken around $z_2$ will be multiplication by
$\e^{2 \pi \I(h_i - h_2 - h_1)}$.

Conversely, to prove \autoref{prop:factorization-strongly-rational}, the main thing we need to 
do is to show that \ti{all} conformal blocks have this kind of
behavior as $z_1 \to z_2$. I think the idea is like this.
First we show that the connection on conformal blocks is \ti{regular singular},
in the sense that the correlation functions satisfy a finite-rank system of differential equations 
which has only first-order poles at the diagonal.
(We saw this directly for our main example \eqref{eq:kz-kohno}.
More generally it is true at least if we consider strongly rational vertex algebras
and insert only ordinary modules.
Note it is certainly \ti{not} true if we consider an ordinary module approaching an 
``irregular module'' $\vrep(u)$: then we would get a higher-order pole.)
It follows then that the correlation functions have at most polynomial growth
at the diagonal (but they could have logs).
Next we \ti{construct} some $\vertex$-module directly from the local behavior 
of the correlation functions at the diagonal. 
Then we use the assumed finiteness properties to 
prove that this $\vertex$-module is ordinary, conclude
it decomposes as a direct sum of ordinary modules, and get the corresponding decomposition 
for the correlation function.
This has been carried out in \fixme{...}.


\subsubsection{Associator}

At any rate, suppose we know \autoref{prop:factorization-strongly-rational}.
Then we consider the space of 4-point blocks with
$\vrep_1$, $\vrep_2$, $\vrep_3$, $W$ inserted,
at points $z_1 < z_2 < z_3$ in $\bbR$ and $\infty$.
When $z_2$ is close to $z_1$ this is
\begin{equation}
  \bigoplus_i \Hom(\vrep_1 \boxtimes \vrep_2, \cS_i) \otimes \Hom(\cS_i \boxtimes \vrep_3, W^*)
\end{equation}
In turn this is equal to\footnote{Indeed, in any finite semisimple abelian category $\cC$, we have $\bigoplus_i \Hom(A, \cS_i) \otimes F(\cS_i) = F(A)$ for any additive functor $F: \cC^\op \to \Vect$.
We apply it here with $A = \vrep_1 \boxtimes \vrep_2$ and $F(A) = \Hom(A \boxtimes \vrep_3, W)$.
To think about it concretely imagine $F(A) = \Hom(A,B)$: 
then it says $\bigoplus_i \Hom(A, \cS_i) \otimes \Hom(\cS_i, B) = \Hom(A,B)$.
To prove that version: we have an obvious map from the left
side to the right; what we need is to invert it. For this
use the canonical decomposition 
$A \simeq \bigoplus_j V_{j,A} \otimes \cS_{j}$;
that induces an isomorphism $\Hom(A, \cS_i) \otimes \Hom(\cS_i, B) \to \bigoplus_j V_{j,A}^* \otimes \Hom(\cS_j, \cS_i) \otimes \Hom(\cS_i, B) = V_{i,A}^* \otimes \Hom(\cS_i, B) = \Hom(V_{i,A} \otimes \cS_i, B)$,
and then taking the direct sum over $i$ gives $\Hom(A,B)$ as desired.

For those who know more about category theory than I do: if I understand
right, this is an example of the co-Yoneda lemma, applied to the coend
$\int^{X \in \cC} \Hom(A,X) \otimes F(X)$ of the functor
$G(X,Y): \cC^{\op} \times \cC \to \Vect$ defined by
$G(X,Y) = \Hom(A,Y) \otimes F(X)$.
}
\begin{equation}
 \Hom((\vrep_1 \boxtimes \vrep_2) \boxtimes \vrep_3, W^*) 
\end{equation}
Similarly when $z_2$ is close to $z_3$ 
we can identify the space of $4$-point blocks with
\begin{equation}
 \Hom(\vrep_1 \boxtimes (\vrep_2 \boxtimes \vrep_3), W^*) \, . 
\end{equation}
Continuation in the $z_2$ parameter then gives an isomorphism
\begin{equation}
 \Hom((\vrep_1 \boxtimes \vrep_2) \boxtimes \vrep_3, W) \to \Hom(\vrep_1 \boxtimes (\vrep_2 \boxtimes \vrep_3), W) \, .
\end{equation}
Having this for every $W$ is equivalent to having
an \ti{associator} map
\begin{equation}
 a_{1,2,3}: (\vrep_1 \boxtimes \vrep_2) \boxtimes \vrep_3 \to \vrep_1 \boxtimes (\vrep_2 \boxtimes \vrep_3) \, .
\end{equation}

What is the concrete data here? Recall that we have the canonical multiplicity vector spaces $V_{ij}^k$, $\dim V_{ij}^k = N_{ij}^k$, 
with a canonical isomorphism
\begin{equation}
  \cS_i \boxtimes \cS_j \simeq \bigoplus_{k} V_{ij}^k \otimes \cS_k \, .
\end{equation}
Then the associator amounts to a collection of linear isomorphisms
\begin{equation}
  F^\ell_{ijk}: \bigoplus_p V^p_{ij} \otimes V^\ell_{pk} \to \bigoplus_q V^\ell_{iq} \otimes V^q_{jk} \, ,
\end{equation}
or more atomically
\begin{equation}
  [F^\ell_{ijk}]_{pq}: V^p_{ij} \otimes V^\ell_{pk} \to V^\ell_{iq} \otimes V^q_{jk} \, .
\end{equation}
They are sometimes called \ti{$F$-matrices}, or \ti{$6j$ symbols} because
of the $6$ indices $ijk\ell{}pq$.
They don't literally become matrices unless we choose bases in
each $V_{ij}^k$.
They satisfy the \ti{pentagon relation} which we will discuss below.

(I should emphasize that this is not something spooky:
the same sort of objects would appear already for the
category of finite-dimensional representations of a compact

We can understand it as coming from a homotopy between paths in configuration space.
The key point is that all of the tensor products we consider can be described in terms of 
conformal blocks where \ti{all insertions are on the real line}, and 
the configuration space of ordered $4$-tuples of points $t_1 < t_2 < t_3 < t_4$ on the real line is simply connected \fixme{draw figure}.)
For instance: the space $\Hom(A \boxtimes (B \boxtimes (C \boxtimes D)), W^*)$ is a space of 5-point conformal blocks
constructed by iterated plumbing, which converges in a region 
where $t_4 - t_3 \ll t_3 - t_2 \ll t_2 - t_1$. 
The other bracketings are similar. Then the associator corresponds to analytic continuation of the correlation functions
from one of these convergence domains to another.

One pretty way of formulating this is: interpret factorization as 
saying that the bundle of conformal blocks extends over a
\ti{compactification} of the moduli of curves, to include 
stable nodal curves. Then the pentagon relation can be formulated
as a homotopy in the moduli space $\overline{\cM_{0,5}}(\bbR)$ of 
real stable curves of genus zero with 5 marked points;
this moduli space is a pentagon, with the 
five bracketings correspond to the five vertices.

\item The braiding and associator together obey two hexagon relations.

\item The coevaluation and evaluation obey zig-zag (straightening) relations, connecting $\coev^L$ and $\ev^L$,
or $\coev^R$ and $\ev^R$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:zigzag-1}
\rho_X\circ(\mathrm{id}_X\boxtimes \ev^L)\circ a_{X,X^*,X}\circ(\coev^L\boxtimes \mathrm{id}_X)\circ \lambda_X^{-1}
=\mathrm{id}_X,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:zigzag-2}
\lambda_{X^*}\circ(\ev^L\boxtimes \mathrm{id}_{X^*})\circ a^{-1}_{X^*,X,X^*}\circ(\mathrm{id}_{X^*}\boxtimes \coev^L)\circ \rho_{X^*}^{-1}
=\mathrm{id}_{X^*}.
\end{equation}
% Right zig-zag (triangle) identities:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:zigzag-3}
\lambda_X\circ(\ev^R\boxtimes \mathrm{id}_X)\circ a^{-1}_{X,X^*,X}\circ(\mathrm{id}_X\boxtimes \coev^R)\circ \rho_X^{-1}
=\mathrm{id}_X,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:zigzag-4}
\rho_{X^*}\circ(\mathrm{id}_{X^*}\boxtimes \ev^R)\circ a_{X^*,X,X^*}\circ(\coev^R\boxtimes \mathrm{id}_{X^*})\circ \lambda_{X^*}^{-1}
=\mathrm{id}_{X^*}.
\end{equation}
We think of these relations as partially fixing the normalization
of $\ev$ and $\coev$, which we left unfixed until now.

we could \ti{define} $\coev^R$ in terms of $\coev^L$ and the
braiding, and similarly we could define $\ev^R$ in terms of
$\ev^L$ and the braiding. We can think of them as
fixing the normalization of $\coev^R$, $\ev^R$ completely
in terms of those of $\coev^L$, $\ev^L$. 
They imply $\ev^L_X \circ \coev^R_X = \ev^R_X \circ \coev^L_X$.

If $\vrep = \vrep(M)$, then we may choose 
$\coev^L_{\vrep}(\bid)$
to be the standard conformal block, which in particular acts
on $m \in M$, $\eta \in M^*$ by $\Phi(m,\eta) = \eta \cdot m$.
If we do so, then 
$\coev^R_{\vrep}(\bid)$ is fixed to \fixme{sign}
$\Phi(m,\eta) = \exp(2 \pi \I h_M) \eta \cdot m$.
\end{example}

we have to live with this convention, and different authors

\item The twist is compatible with the braiding and associator,
in the sense that:
\[
\theta_{\bid}=\id_{\bid} \, , \quad
a_{X,Y,Z}\circ \theta_{(X\boxtimes Y)\boxtimes Z}

\theta_{X\boxtimes (Y\boxtimes Z)}\circ a_{X,Y,Z} \, , \quad
\theta_{X\boxtimes Y}

Let us discuss how to prove the last identity here.
The intuitive picture is: acting with a twist on $X \boxtimes Y$
means applying $\exp(2 \pi \I L_0)$ to the whole composite, which
amounts to applying it to each of $X$ and $Y$ separately, plus
moving the insertion point at $1$ around a circle around $0$.
To turn this into an actual proof, we need to think about how
we even define the action of $L_0$ (or any vertex
algebra element for that matter) on a tensor product.
As usual the answer has to be in terms of the universal property:
it's enough to give the action of $L_0$ on all conformal blocks.
We use the formula
$L_0 = \oint z T(z) \de z$, and contour deformation:
this shows that with insertions at $0$ and $w$,
$L_0$ acts by $L_0 \otimes \id + \id \otimes L_0 + w \id \otimes L_{-1}$. 
The third vector field is $w \partial_w$, the generator of dilations.

\item The twist is compatible with evaluation and coevaluation, in the sense that twisting on either leg is equivalent:\footnote{This condition is sometimes written more succinctly as $\theta_{X^*} = (\theta_X)^*$,}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:twist-coev-ev-1}
(\theta_X\boxtimes \id_{X^*})\circ \coev_X^{L}
=
(\id_X\boxtimes \theta_{X^*})\circ \coev_X^{L},
\qquad
\ev_X^{L}\circ(\theta_{X^*}\boxtimes \id_X)
=
\ev_X^{L}\circ(\id_{X^*}\boxtimes \theta_X).
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:twist-coev-ev-2}
(\theta_{X^*}\boxtimes \id_X)\circ \coev_X^{R}
=
(\id_{X^*}\boxtimes \theta_X)\circ \coev_X^{R},
\qquad
\ev_X^{R}\circ(\theta_X\boxtimes \id_{X^*})
=
\ev_X^{R}\circ(\id_X\boxtimes \theta_{X^*}).
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}


All of these properties together make $\cC$ into 
a \ti{premodular tensor category} or \ti{ribbon fusion category}.
I hope I have now listed all the axioms. For much more on these
kinds of categories see e.g. \cite{Mueger2012,BK2001,EGNO2015}.

One observation you quickly make when you start playing with $\cC$
is: the objects $a$, $\lambda$, $\rho$ are needed to make the 
equations sensible, but you can always guess where you need to put
them, and so it should be possible to use a reduced notation where
you simply omit them. This is indeed the case:

\begin{thm}[Coherence theorem] Every premodular tensor category $\cC$ is equivalent to
one which is \ti{strict}, meaning that $X \boxtimes (Y \boxtimes Z) = (X \boxtimes Y) \boxtimes Z$ on the nose, similarly $\bid \boxtimes X = X = X \boxtimes \bid$, and $a$, $\lambda$, $\rho$ are all identity maps.
\end{thm}

This theorem is stated at least in \cite{Turaev1992ModularCategories}
and the version for monoidal categories is in \cite{EGNO2015}.
Here ``equivalence'' means it's an equivalence of linear categories,
compatible in an appropriate way with the monoidal structure, 
braiding and twist.
The upshot is that for some purposes it is safe to pretend that the 
category $\cC$ is indeed strict.


\subsection{Link invariants}

Now given any premodular tensor category $\cC$
we can consider oriented ribbon link diagrams $L$,
where each component is labeled by an object $\vrep$ of $\cC$ (reversing orientation
swaps $\vrep \to \vrep^*$).

Such a diagram gives a morphism $Z_L: \bid \to \bid$ in $\cC$:
cups/caps give $\coev_\vrep$ and $\ev_\vrep$, 
twists/kinks give $\theta_\vrep^{\pm 1}$,
crossings give $b^\pm_{\vrep,\vrep'}$.
We can also sprinkle in associators as necessary, or maybe better,
pass to a strictification of $\cC$, so we don't need them.

Since $\Hom(\bid,\bid) = \bbC$ 
we can think of $Z_L$ as a number associated to the 
link diagram. 
% (Or, perhaps more concretely, we can think of the link diagram as defining an operator which maps the
%  space of conformal blocks on $S^2$ to itself.)
Now the nontrivial claim: $Z_L$
is actually an invariant of the ribbon link, not only of the link
diagram.
This amounts to checking that it is invariant under various moves.
All of the needed invariance follow from the coherences we have
already discussed:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Zig-zag identities (pulling a strand straight): use \eqref{eq:zigzag-1}-\eqref{eq:zigzag-4}.
\item Reidemeister I: see an exercise below (this is the most interesting one).
\item Reidemeister II: cancel $b_{X,Y}$ with $b^{-1}_{X,Y}$.
\item Reidemeister III: see an exercise below.
\item Pitchfork identities (pulling a strand across a cup or cap): see an exercise below.
\item Moving twists across cups/caps: use \eqref{eq:twist-coev-ev-1}-\eqref{eq:twist-coev-ev-2}.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{exercise}
Prove the ``pitchfork'' identities:
\begin{align*}

=\coev_X\boxtimes \id_Y,\\
% &\text{(cup pitchfork, other side)}\\
&(b_{X,Y}\boxtimes \id_{X^*})\circ(\id_X\boxtimes b_{X^*,Y})\circ(\coev_X\boxtimes \id_Y)
=\id_Y\boxtimes \coev_X,\\
% &\text{(cap pitchfork)}\\
&(\id_Y\boxtimes \ev_X)\circ(b_{X^*,Y}\boxtimes \id_X)\circ(\id_{X^*}\boxtimes b_{X,Y})
=\ev_X\boxtimes \id_Y,\\
% &\text{(cap pitchfork, other side)}\\
&(\ev_X\boxtimes \id_Y)\circ(\id_{X^*}\boxtimes b_{Y,X})\circ(b_{Y,X^*}\boxtimes \id_X)
=\id_Y\boxtimes \ev_X.
\end{align*}
\end{exercise}

\subsection{Examples}

Let me describe what these structures actually are in two baby examples.

\begin{example}

Take $\vertex = L_1(\fsl_2)$.
In this case we just have simple modules $\cS_0, \cS_1$.
The only nontrivial fusion rule is $\cS_1 \boxtimes \cS_1 \simeq \cS_0$.

The only nontrivial $R$-matrix is $R^{0}_{11} = \I$, and 
the twist is $\theta_1 = \I$. (We can calculate the latter 
directly, since $\cS_1$ has highest 
weight $h_1 = C_2(M_1) / 2(k+h^\vee) = \frac32 / 2(3) = \frac14$;
then this determines the former, using the exercise above.)

(Note it means that these particles are ``semions,'' like half of a 
fermion: when you exchange them you get a factor of $\I$.)

\fixme{should also discuss $F$-matrices, carefully; all scalars $\pm 1$ but which?}

\end{example}


\begin{example}

Take $\vertex = L_2(\fsl_2)$. This case is a bit more interesting.\footnote{The fusion rules we get here match with what physicists call ``Ising anyon'' statistics.
But, warning: I think the $R$-matrix is not that of the usual
Ising model.}

We have three simple modules $\cS_0, \cS_1, \cS_2$,
with conformal weights
\begin{equation}
  h_0 = 0, \quad h_1 = \frac{3}{16}, \quad h_2 = \frac{1}{2} \, ,
\end{equation}
and corresponding twists
\begin{equation}
  \theta_0 = 1, \quad \theta_1 = \e^{3 \pi \I/8}, \quad \theta_2 = -1 \, .
\end{equation}
The nontrivial fusion rules are
\begin{equation}
  \cS_1 \boxtimes \cS_1 \simeq \cS_0 \oplus \cS_2, \quad \cS_1 \boxtimes \cS_2 \simeq \cS_1, \quad \cS_2 \boxtimes \cS_2 \simeq \cS_0 \, . 
\end{equation}

Now we describe the $R$ and $F$ matrices.
They will depend on choices of bases in the nontrivial invariant spaces
$V_{ij}^k$; thus we have $(\bbC^\times)^4$ worth of ambiguity.

Note that $V_{ij}^k$ is a space of 3-point blocks on the sphere,
identified with the \ti{classical} multiplicity space
$H_{ij}^k = \Hom_\fg(M_i \otimes M_j, M_k)$. So, concretely,
we can fix all the ambiguity by choosing bases in $H_{ij}^k$.

The $R$-matrices are
\begin{equation}
\quad R^0_{11} = \e^{5 \pi \I/8}, \quad R^2_{11} = \e^{\pi \I / 8}, \quad R^1_{12} = R^1_{21} = \I, \quad R^0_{22} = -1.
\end{equation}
Here $R^0_{11} = - \exp(\pi \I (h_0-2 h_1))$,
$R^2_{11} = \exp( \pi \I (h_2 - 2 h_1))$,
and $R^0_{22} = \exp( \pi \I (h_0 - 2 h_2))$.
The exponential factor in $R_{ii}^k$
comes from the 
half-monodromy of $z^{h_k - 2 h_i}$.
The extra $\pm$ sign is the eigenvalue
of the swap acting on the \ti{classical} multiplicity space $H_{ii}^k$.
Finally, the choice of $R^1_{12}$ is a gauge choice, which then fixes
$R^1_{21}$ since $R^1_{12} R^1_{21} = \exp(2 \pi \I h_2) = -1$.

Now for the $F$-matrices.
There are $10$ scalar $F$-matrices, e.g.
\begin{equation}
  F_{212}^1: V_{21}^1 \otimes V_{12}^1 \to V_{12}^1 \otimes V_{21}^1, \qquad F_{121}^2: V_{12}^1 \otimes V_{11}^2 \to V_{11}^2 \otimes V_{21}^1
\end{equation}
both of which are determined by the hexagon relations:
$F_{212}^1 = -1$ and $F_{121}^2 = \I R^1_{12} = -1$.
Note the first is fully gauge invariant while the second depended
on a gauge choice.
If we maintain this same choice, the $8$ other scalar
$F$-matrices are all $+1$.

There is one 2-dimensional fusion space 
\begin{equation}
 \cS_1 \boxtimes \cS_1 \boxtimes \cS_1 \simeq \cS_1 \oplus \cS_1 \, . 
\end{equation}
It gives rise to a single $F$-matrix,
\begin{equation}
 F_{111}^1: (V_{11}^{0} \otimes V_{01}^1 \oplus V_{11}^2 \otimes V_{21}^1) \to (V_{10}^{1} \otimes V_{11}^0 \oplus V_{12}^1 \otimes V_{11}^2) \, .
\end{equation}
% Again using the hexagon relations this matrix is determined,
% \begin{equation}
%   F_{111}^1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
% \end{equation}
% (Here the diagonal entries are canonical once we have fixed
% the $R$-matrices; the off-diagonal entries involve an extra
% gauge choice.)

Let us try to calculate $F_{111}^1$ directly from the vertex algebra.
Actually we 
will do the computation a bit more generally, for any $k \ge 2$ instead
of just $k=2$.
We consider conformal blocks on the sphere with 4 marked points at
$0, x, 1, \infty$,
all carrying the representation $\vrep(M_1)$, where we recall $M_1$
is the fundamental representation of $\fsl_2$.
Now we consider varying $x$.
The connection in the bundle of conformal blocks then
takes the concrete form\footnote{More generally, with $n$ punctures in
the finite plane and one more at $\infty$ we would write the operator $\partial_{z_i} - \frac{1}{k + h^\vee} \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{T_{ij}}{z_i - z_j}$ --- just like \eqref{eq:kz-kohno}.}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:kz-4-punctures}
  \left[  \partial_{x} - \frac{1}{k+2} \left(  \frac{T_{12}}{x} + \frac{T_{23}}{x-1} \right) \right] \psi = 0 \, ,
\end{equation}
acting on the classical invariant subspace 
$H \subset M_1^{\otimes 4}$, which has dimension $2$.
(Happily, this $2$ matches the dimension of the space of 
$4$-point conformal blocks, i.e. in this example 
there is no additional truncation
to worry about.)

For $x$ near $0$ ($\abs{x}<1$), 
given a vector $\varphi \in H_{11}^a \otimes H_{a1}^1$,
we can get a conformal block $\psi$ by plumbing.
What the plumbing would give us directly is a power series in $x$,
\begin{equation}
 \psi = \sum_n x^n \psi_n = x^{\lambda_a} \varphi(e_i,e^i) + \cdots 
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_a = h_a - 2 h_1$, so $\lambda_0 = - \frac{3}{2(k+2)}$ and
$\lambda_2 = \frac{1}{2(k+2)}$.
The higher terms are the contributions from descendants in $\cS_a$.
Happily we don't need to write out the whole power series:
it is determined from $\varphi$, by the differential equation
\eqref{eq:kz-4-punctures}.
Indeed, we could have constructed the same power series by 
Frobenius's method for constructing
solutions of ODE around regular singularities: every eigenvector $\varphi$
of the residue matrix $\frac{1}{k+2} T_{12}$ gives a solution
of the ODE. (To be precise, this is true as long as no two eigenvalues
differ by an integer; that's the case here, since their difference is $\frac{2}{k+2}$.)
The fusion channels $H_{11}^a \otimes H_{a1}^1 \subset H$ 
indeed give eigenlines of $T_{12}$.

Now suppose $x$ is near $1$. Then given a vector
$\varphi' \in H_{1a}^1 \otimes H_{11}^a$ we again get a conformal
block $\psi'$ by plumbing, now of the form
\begin{equation}
 \psi' = \sum_n (1-x)^n \psi_n = (1-x)^{\lambda_a} \varphi'(e_i,e^i) + \cdots 
\end{equation}
Again these blocks can be built by Frobenius's method, now starting from 
the eigenvector $\varphi'$ of $T_{13}$.

Now we look at the space of solutions of \eqref{eq:kz-4-punctures}
on the interval $(0,1)$. We have identified this space 
both with $\oplus_a H_{11}^a \otimes H_{a1}^1$ and with 
$\oplus_a H_{1a}^1 \otimes H_{11}^a$, so we get a map between these two:
that is the $F$-matrix $F_{111}^1$.

To go further we should fix our bases. Let's choose generators as
follows. Define $M_1$ to be the standard representation, 
and $M_2 = \Sym^2 M_1$.
Let $\eps: M_1 \otimes M_1 \to \bbC$ be the standard
antisymmetric pairing.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For $H^0_{11}$ use the antisymmetric pairing $\eps$.
  \item For $H^2_{11}$ take the projection to the symmetric part.
  \item For $H^1_{01}$ and $H^1_{10}$ use the identity map.
  \item For $H^1_{21}$ use the intertwiner
  \begin{equation}
    m((uv) \otimes w) = \frac{1}{\sqrt3} (\eps(v,w) u + \eps(u,w) v) \, . 
  \end{equation}
  \item For $H^1_{12}$ use the intertwiner
  \begin{equation}
    m(u \otimes (vw)) = \frac{1}{\sqrt3} (\eps(u,v) w + \eps(u,w) v) \, . 
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Once we have made these choices, everything else is fixed.
The $\phi_a: M_1^3 \to M_a$ are
\begin{equation}
  \phi_0(u,v,w) = \eps(u,v) w, \quad \phi_2(u,v,w) = \frac{1}{\sqrt3} (\eps(v,w) u + \eps(u,w) v) \, ,
\end{equation}
and similarly $\phi'_a: M_1^3 \to M_a$ are
\begin{equation}
  \phi'_0(u,v,w) = \eps(v,w) u, \quad \phi'_2(u,v,w) = \frac{1}{\sqrt3} (\eps(u,v) w + \eps(u,w) v) \, .
\end{equation}

\end{equation}
and relative to the basis $(\phi_0, \phi_2)$ we have
\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

matrix equation in the plane, with regular singularities at $0$, $1$
and $\infty$. Such equations can always be reduced to hypergeometric

relates the monodromy eigenbasis at $0$ to the monodromy eigenbasis 
at $1$. That's what we need here.

\end{equation}
As $k \to \infty$ this recovers the classical change-of-basis
(as it must since in that limit the equation just becomes
$\partial_z \psi = 0$.)

\subsection{Quantum dimensions and quantum traces}

Now how to calculate the link invariants in practice?
One simple example of such a link invariant would be the case of the unknot.
The invariant for an unknot labeled with $X$ is 
called the \ti{quantum dimension} $d_X$. It has
\begin{equation}
  d_X = \ev_X^R \circ \coev_X^L = \ev_X^L \circ \coev_X^R \, .
\end{equation}

There is a direct way of computing the 
quantum dimension if we already know the $F$-matrices:

\begin{prop}
Choose bases for $V_{XX^*}^\bid = \Hom(\bid, X \boxtimes X^*)$ and $V_{X^*X}^\bid = \Hom(\bid, X^* \boxtimes X)$
given by $\coev_X^L(\bid)$ and $\coev_X^R(\bid)$ respectively.
Then we have 
\begin{equation}
  d_X = 1 / (F_{X X^* X}^X)_{\bid \bid}
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Call $(X \boxtimes X^*) \boxtimes X$ and $X \boxtimes (X^* \boxtimes X)$
respectively the $t$ and $s$ channels.
Let $\psi_t^\mu$ and $\psi_s^\mu$ be the $t$- and $s$-channel bases of
$\Hom(X\boxtimes X^*\boxtimes X,X)$ induced by the chosen bases of the multiplicity spaces.
% Since our basis vectors in
% \[
% V_{XX^*}^{\bid}=\Hom(\bid,X\boxtimes X^*),
% \qquad
% V_{X^*X}^{\bid}=\Hom(\bid,X^*\boxtimes X)
% \]
% are $\coev_X^R$ and $\coev_X^L$, the corresponding dual basis vectors in
% \[
% \Hom(X\boxtimes X^*,\bid),\qquad \Hom(X^*\boxtimes X,\bid)
% \]
% are $d_X^{-1}\ev_X^L$ and $d_X^{-1}\ev_X^R$.
We have
\[
\psi_t^{\bid}=\frac1{d_X}\,(\ev_X^R\boxtimes \id_X),
\qquad
\psi_s^{\bid}=\frac1{d_X}\,(\id_X\boxtimes \ev_X^L).
\]
By definition of the $F$-matrix,
\[
\psi_t^{\bid}=\sum_\mu (F_{XX^*X}^X)_{\mu\bid}\,\psi_s^\mu .
\]

Now let
\[
e=\frac1{d_X}\,\coev_X^R\circ \ev_X^L \in \End(X^*\boxtimes X).
\]
We have $e^2=e$, so $e$ is the projector onto the
$\bid$-summand in $X^*\boxtimes X$. Therefore
\[
\psi_s^\mu\circ (\id_X\boxtimes e)=
\begin{cases}
\psi_s^{\bid}, & \mu=\bid,\\
0, & \mu\neq \bid.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus
\[
\psi_t^{\bid}\circ(\id_X\boxtimes e)
=
(F_{XX^*X}^X)_{\bid\bid}\,\psi_s^{\bid}.
\]

On the other hand,
\begin{align*}
\psi_t^{\bid}\circ(\id_X\boxtimes e)
&=
\frac1{d_X^2}\,
(\ev_X^R\boxtimes \id_X)\circ
(\id_X\boxtimes \coev_X^R\circ \ev_X^L) \\
&=
\frac1{d_X^2}\,
(\id_X\boxtimes \ev_X^L)
=
\frac1{d_X}\,\psi_s^{\bid},
\end{align*}
using the zig-zag identity.
% \[
% (\ev_X^L\boxtimes \id_X)\circ(\id_X\boxtimes \coev_X^L)=\id_X.
% \]
Comparing the two expressions, we obtain
\[
(F_{XX^*X}^X)_{\bid\bid}=\frac1{d_X},
\]
as desired.
\end{proof}

There is a little subtlety about self-dual objects:

\begin{defn}
The \ti{transpose} of an element $f \in \Hom(X,Y)$
is an element $f^t \in \Hom(Y^*, X^*)$ 
defined by
\[
f^t
=
(\ev_Y^R\boxtimes \id_{X^*})
\circ
(\id_{Y^*}\boxtimes f\boxtimes \id_{X^*})
\circ
(\id_{Y^*}\boxtimes \coev_X^L) \, .
\] \,
\end{defn}
The transpose obeys (and is characterized by)
\[
\ev_Y^L\circ(f\boxtimes \id_{Y^*})
=
\ev_X^L\circ(\id_X\boxtimes f^t), \quad \ev_Y^R\circ(\id_{Y^*}\boxtimes f)
=
\ev_X^R\circ(f^t\boxtimes \id_X).
\]
\input{figures/transpose-identity}

Suppressing the pivotal identifications $X\simeq X^{**}$ and $Y\simeq Y^{**}$, we have
\[
f^{tt}=f.
\]
We also have the canonical isomorphism
\[
\Hom(X,Y^*)  \simeq  \Hom(X\boxtimes Y,\bid),
\qquad
f \longmapsto \ev_Y^L \circ (f\boxtimes \id_Y)
\]
Composing with this, we can define the transpose of
$\phi \in \Hom(X \boxtimes Y, \bid)$ 
as
$\phi^t \in \Hom(Y \boxtimes X, \bid)$.

\begin{defn}
Suppose $X$ is a simple object with $X \simeq X^*$. 
Consider the $1$-dimensional vector space $\Hom(X,X^*) \simeq \Hom(X \boxtimes X, \bid)$.
It has an involution $\phi \mapsto \phi^t$.
This involution acts as a scalar $\nu_X \in \{ \pm 1 \}$, called
the \ti{Frobenius-Schur indicator} of $X$.
\end{defn}

\begin{example}
The classical example arises when $\cC$ is the category of 
representations of a group, say a finite group or a compact Lie group.
Then, for simple $X$ which is self-dual, there is a map of representations
\begin{equation}
  X \otimes X \to \bbC
\end{equation}
(invariant bilinear pairing), unique up to scalar multiple, 
which could be either symmetric or
antisymmetric. We have $\nu_X = +1 $ if it is symmetric,
$-1$ if antisymmetric.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For $L_k(\fg)$ the Frobenius-Schur indicator is inherited from
the finite-dimensional modules. In particular,
for $L_k(\fsl_2)$ every simple object $\cS_k$ is self-dual,
and $\nu_k = (-1)^k$.
\end{example}

\end{exercise}

\begin{prop}
Suppose $X$ is simple and self-dual. Then
\[
(F_{XXX}^X)_{\bid\bid}
=
\nu_X\,(F_{XX^*X}^X)_{\bid\bid} \, .
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}

Choose a basis vector
\[
q\in V_{XX}^{\bid}=\Hom(\bid,X\boxtimes X),
\]
which we will use for the vacuum channel in both copies of $V_{XX}^{\bid}$ in the definition of $F_{XXX}^X$.
Let
\[
p\in \Hom(X\boxtimes X,\bid)
\]
be the dual basis vector, so that $p\circ q=\id_{\bid}$.
$p$ determines a map $u: X \to X^*$, by 
\[
p=\ev_X^L\circ (u \boxtimes \id_X),
\]
which then has
\[
p^t=\ev_X^R\circ (\id_X \boxtimes u).
\]
Let
\[
T:\Hom(X\boxtimes X^*\boxtimes X,X)\longrightarrow \Hom(X\boxtimes X\boxtimes X,X)
\]
be the isomorphism given by precomposition with $d_X \id_X\boxtimes u\boxtimes \id_X$.
Applying $T$ to the vacuum basis vectors we 
used for $F_{XX^*X}^X$ gives
\[
T\!\left(\frac1{d_X}\,\ev_X^R\boxtimes \id_X\right)
= p^t \boxtimes \id_X, \qquad
T\!\left(\frac1{d_X}\,\id_X\boxtimes \ev_X^L\right)
=
\id_X\boxtimes p \, .
\]
On the other hand, the vacuum basis vectors for $F_{XXX}^X$ determined by $q$ are
\[
p\boxtimes \id_X,
\qquad
\id_X\boxtimes p.
\]
Thus the change-of-basis coefficients for the two bases differ by
$\nu_X$, giving the desired result.
\end{proof}
\begin{cor}
Suppose $X \simeq X^*$.
Choose a basis for $V^1_{XX}$ once and for all.
Then we have
\begin{equation}
  d_X = \nu_X/(F^X_{XXX})_{\bid \bid} \, .
\end{equation}
\end{cor}

\begin{example}
Applying this formula to $L_k(\fsl_2)$ we get
\begin{equation} \label{eq:qdim-1}
 d_1 = [2]_q = q + q^{-1} \, . 
\end{equation}
For $k = 2$ this is $d_1 = \sqrt2$ as we expected.
Note that $d_1$ 
matches with the Jones polynomial of the unknot,
in blackboard framing.
\end{example}

of quantum dimensions:

\begin{exercise}
Show that the quantum dimensions obey the relations

\end{equation}

How to calculate the quantum dimensions? 
For any object $X$ consider the matrix $N_X$ representing
fusion with simple objects, $(N_X)_{jk} = \dim V_{X \otimes \cS_j}^k$. 
Then the vector $(d_i)$ is an eigenvector of the matrix $N_X$, with 
eigenvalue $d_X$.
In general this isn't quite enough to determine $(d_i)$.
But in the unitary situation it is, because of the following:

\begin{prop} If $\cC$ is unitary, then all $d_i > 0$.
\end{prop}

\begin{example}
In \fixme{...} we have 
\begin{equation}
  N_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad N_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad N_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
Diagonalizing these matrices we see that the only positive eigenvalues are
$(d_0,d_1,d_2) = (1,\sqrt 2,1)$; and sure enough, this vector
is a simultaneous eigenvector of all three matrices.
\end{example}

More generally we also have the quantum trace:
given $f: X \to X$ we define
\begin{equation}
  \Tr f = \ev^R_X \circ (f \otimes \id_{X^*}) \circ \coev^L_X = \ev_X^L \circ (\id_{X^*} \otimes f) \circ \coev_X^R \, .
\end{equation}


\subsection{Skein relations}

Consider the case of $L_k(\fsl_2)$.
Let $X = S_1$. We are interested in links colored by the object $X$.
We would like to relate overcrossings to undercrossings,
i.e. $b_{X,X}$ to $b^{-1}_{X,X}$.
We have, with $Y = S_2$,
\begin{equation}
  X \boxtimes X = \bid \oplus Y
\end{equation}
and the braiding $b_{X,X}$ is diagonal with respect to this
decomposition, with eigenvalues\footnote{By $q^{1/2}$ we mean specifically $\exp(\pi \I / (2(k + 2)))$. Some authors would call this $A$.}
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_\bid = -\exp(-\pi \I (2 h_X)) = -q^{-3/2}, \qquad  \lambda_Y = \exp(-\pi \I(2 h_X - h_Y)) = q^{1/2} \, .
\end{equation}
Similarly we can write the action of $b^{-1}_{X,X}$, and the action of $\id_{X \boxtimes X}$.
Since these are $3$ operators acting on a $2$-dimensional space they must obey a linear relation,
and we work it out to
\begin{equation} \label{eq:skein-relation}
  q^{1/2} b_{X,X} - q^{-1/2} b^{-1}_{X,X} = (q - q^{-1}) \id \, .
\end{equation}
This is an example of a \ti{skein relation}. \fixme{fig}

Using \eqref{eq:skein-relation} and \eqref{eq:qdim-1}, together with the ribbon isotopy invariance,
we can evaluate the invariants $Z_L$ for oriented link diagrams $L$ colored by the representation $X$.
(The idea is that we can use the skein relation to replace overcrossings by undercrossings and
vice versa, modulo links with fewer crossings; but by changing crossings appropriately we can always reduce
the knot to a bunch of unknots, so we can evaluate it.)
The resulting $Z_L$ is an invariant of the ribbon link. We can modify it slightly to get an invariant of 
the link alone:
\begin{prop}[$L_k(\fsl_2)$ invariants vs Jones polynomials]
Let $J(L)$ denote the Jones polynomial of the link $L$. Then
$Z_L = q^{3w(L)/2} J(L)$, where $w(L)$ is the \ti{writhe} of $L$, i.e.
the number of overcrossings minus undercrossings.
\end{prop}

A side remark, for those who know: $Z_L$ is very similar to the Kauffman bracket invariant. This is a little 
puzzling, since the Kauffman bracket invariant is defined by an \ti{unoriented} skein relation,
one which you can't really even write down if you use oriented links.
You might think that it is possible to use unoriented links, since $X \simeq X^*$. But the
fact that $\nu_X = -1$ makes this tricky: you have to insert ``coupons'' on the links representing the
isomorphism $X \simeq X^*$, and you can get a sign when you slide a coupon past a $\coev$ or $\ev$.
There is a fix: you can modify the category $\cC$, changing the twist $\theta_X \to \nu_X \theta_X$; 
after so doing you will have $\nu_X = 1$ for all simple objects. In this modified category you can really
use unoriented links without fear, and then you get the Kauffman bracket relation on the nose.
See the notorious \cite{Tingley2017MinusSign}, and \cite{SnyderTingley2009HalfTwist}.


\subsection{Verlinde loop operators}

Above we discussed colored oriented ribbon links $L \subset \bbR^3$.

More generally by the same construction, a colored oriented ribbon link
in $C \times I$ produces an operator on the space $\Conf(C)$ of conformal blocks. In the case
of a simple loop in $C$, this is sometimes called a \ti{Verlinde loop operator}.
The Verlinde loop operators generate a \ti{skein algebra} which we call $\Sk(\vertex, C)$.
In the case $\vertex = L_k(\fsl_2)$ we wrote explicitly the relations defining the skein

So each space $\Conf(C)$ is naturally a representation of $\Conf(C)$.



\subsection{Irregular Verlinde operators}

When we insert irregular modules on $C$, there is an additional possibility:
we can define \ti{irregular Verlinde operators}. I don't know how to set

We revisit the setup of \autoref{ex:kz-2-reg-1-irreg}.
So, suppose $\vertex = V^k(\fg)$.
We take $C = \bbC \bbP^1$, with marked points at $z_1$, $z_2$, and $\infty$.
At $z_1$ and $z_2$ we insert parabolic 
Verma modules $\vrep(M_1)$ and $\vrep(M_2)$
while at $\infty$ we insert the irregular module $\vrep(u)$, for some $u \in \fg$ (identified with $\fg^*$ using the invariant pairing $\kappa$.)
Again, the subspace $M_1 \otimes M_2 \otimes \bbC \vac_u \subset \vrep(M_1) \otimes \vrep(M_2) \otimes \vrep(u)$ 
represents $\Coinv(C)$ for every $z_1$, $z_2$.
The computation we did there is now modified by an extra term: we get
% \begin{equation}
%  o(f J^a) = J^a_{-1} \otimes \bid \otimes \bid + \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{\bid \otimes J^a_{n} \otimes \bid}{(z_1 - z_2)^{n+1}} + \sum_{n \ge 0} z_1^n (\bid \otimes \bid \otimes J^a_{n+1})
% \end{equation}
% Acting on $M_1 \otimes M_2 \otimes \bbC \vac_u$
% this reduces to
% \begin{equation}
%   J^a_{-1} \otimes \bid \otimes \bid + \frac{\bid \otimes J^a_{0} \otimes \bid}{z_1 - z_2} + \bid \otimes \bid \otimes u^a
% \end{equation}
% Thus the image of this operator lies in $K(C)$,
% so modulo $K(C)$
% we can replace the action of $J_{-1}$ at $z_1$
% with the action of $J_0$ at $z_2$ plus the action of $u$ at $\infty$. 
% This gives
\begin{equation}
  L^{(1)}_{-1} = - \frac{1}{k + h^\vee} \left( \sum_{a=1}^{\dim \fg} \frac{J^a_0 \otimes J^a_0 \otimes \bid}{z_1 - z_2} - u \otimes \bid \otimes \bid \right)
\end{equation}
Now fix $z_1 = z$, $z_2 = 0$. Then we get the ODE
\begin{equation} \label{eq:kz-irregular}
  \partial_z + \frac{1}{\hbar}  \left( u \otimes \bid + \frac{T}{z} \right)
\end{equation}
acting on $\Coinv(C) \simeq M_1 \otimes M_2$, where we introduced
the notation $\hbar = k + h^\vee$.

This ODE has a regular singularity at $z=0$ as we have discussed
before, but also an irregular singularity at $z=\infty$.
How can we think about its solutions near the irregular singularity? 
A crude first approximation would be to forget the $T / z$ term.
In that approximation we could write solutions in the form
\begin{equation} \label{eq:naive-asymptotics}
  \psi = \exp(-u z / \hbar) \, v \otimes w
\end{equation}
Now following the spirit of Frobenius's method one might try to build
\ti{actual} solutions which behave ``like'' \eqref{eq:naive-asymptotics}
as $z \to \infty$, by some kind of series expansion around $z = \infty$.
If you try this, you discover a problem: the equation actually does not
have solutions which behave this way, uniformly in $z$. The best we 
can do is to pick some \ti{sector} and ask for these asymptotics
as $z \to \infty$ in that sector.

Here is a baby example of the same phenomenon. We
consider the Bessel equation
\[
z^{2}y''+zy'+(z^{2}-\nu^{2})y=0 \, .
\]
Formally expanding around $\infty$ we expect solutions of the form\footnote{More exactly
\[
y^R_\pm(z)=z^{-1/2}\,e^{\pm \I z}\,\sum_{k\ge 0} a_k^{(\pm)}\,z^{-k},\qquad a_0^{(\pm)}=1,
\]
with
\[
a_k^{(\pm)}=\frac{(\pm \I)^k}{k!\,8^k}\prod_{m=1}^k\Bigl(4\nu^2-(2m-1)^2\Bigr).
\]
So
\[
y^R_\pm(z)=z^{-1/2}e^{\pm \I z}\Biggl(
1
\ \pm\ \frac{\I(4\nu^2-1)}{8z}
\ -\ \frac{(4\nu^2-1)(4\nu^2-9)}{2!\,(8z)^2}
\ \mp\ \frac{\I(4\nu^2-1)(4\nu^2-9)(4\nu^2-25)}{3!\,(8z)^3}
\ + O(z^{-4})
\Biggr).
\]
}
\[ \label{eq:series-bessel}
y^{R}_{\pm}(z)\sim z^{-1/2}e^{\pm \I z}\Bigl(1+O(z^{-1})\Bigr)\qquad (z\to +\infty),
\]
and
\[ \label{eq:series-bessel-2}
y^{L}_{\pm}(z)\sim (-z)^{-1/2}e^{\pm \I z}\Bigl(1+O(z^{-1})\Bigr)\qquad (z\to -\infty).
\]

These series are not convergent.
Nevertheless, there exist unique solutions $(y^{R}_{+},y^{R}_{-})$ (resp. $(y^{L}_{+},y^{L}_{-})$) which have the
asymptotics \eqref{eq:series-bessel} as $z \to \infty$ (resp. \eqref{eq:series-bessel-2} as $z \to -\infty$). 
The Stokes phenomenon is the fact that 
these solutions do \emph{not} analytically 
continue to one another.
Continuing in the upper half-plane gives
\[
\binom{y^{L}_{+}}{y^{L}_{-}}
=
S^{+}\binom{y^{R}_{+}}{y^{R}_{-}},
\qquad
S^{+}=
\begin{pmatrix}
-\I & 2\cos(\pi\nu)\\
0 & -\I
\end{pmatrix},
\]
while continuing in the lower half-plane gives
\[
\binom{y^{R}_{+}}{y^{R}_{-}}
=
S^{-}\binom{y^{L}_{+}}{y^{L}_{-}},
\qquad
S^{-}=
\begin{pmatrix}
-\I & 0\\
2\cos(\pi\nu) & -\I
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The diagonal entries here reflect the \ti{formal} monodromy,
i.e. the naive analytic continuation we would expect just by looking
at the formal series solution.
The product $S^+ S^-$ gives the actual monodromy.

We meet a similar situation for our equation \eqref{eq:kz-irregular},
but more complicated because here there are blocks of solutions with the
same exponential coefficient.

To be concrete, let us specialize to the situation where 
$\fg = \fsl_N$, and $u = \I \diag(u_1, \dots, u_N)$ with all $u_i$ real
and $u_1 < u_2 < \cdots < u_N$.
Further specialize so that $M_1$ is the fundamental representation
and write $M = M_2$.
Then, for each $m \in M$ we have $N$ distinguished solutions
$\psi^{L,R}_i(m)$, which have roughly
\begin{equation}
  \psi^{L,R}_i(m,z) \sim \exp(-\I u_i z / \hbar) \, e_i \otimes m
\end{equation}
as $z \to \pm \infty$.
Now we have again Stokes phenomenon, now with the Stokes matrices 
$S^\pm$ organized into $N \times N$ block matrices,
whose entries are valued in $\End(M)$.
The remarkable fact\footnote{which I learned from Xiaomeng Xu and Yan Zhou}
proven in \cite{Xu2020StokesQuantumGroups}
is that these Stokes matrix entries give a representation of 
the quantum group $U_q(\fgl_N)$ on $M$.

For instance consider the case $\fg = \fsl_2$.
Then we have
\begin{equation}
  S^+ = \begin{pmatrix}  k & e \\ 0 & k^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \, , \qquad
  S^- = \begin{pmatrix}  k & 0 \\ f & k^{-1} \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
for some operators $e, f, k \in \End(M)$.
Then define
\begin{equation}
  K = k, \quad E = \frac{K^{-1} e}{q^{-1} - q}, \quad F = \frac{f K}{q - q^{-1}} \, .
\end{equation}
These operators obey the relations of $U_q(\fsl_2)$:
\begin{equation}
K E K^{-1} = q^2 E, \qquad K F K^{-1} = q^{-2} F, \qquad [E,F] = \frac{K - K^{-1}}{q - q^{-1}} \, .  
\end{equation}

\fixme{try to prove it using skein relations!}

This is a theorem about Stokes matrices, which can be formulated
and proved without mentioning conformal blocks. But
we think of it as an analogue of the notion of Verlinde operator.
We start with a conformal block. Then we create a new insertion of the 
module $\vrep(M_1)$ near the irregular module at $z = + \infty$.
There seem to be $N$ different
``channels'' in which to create this insertion, corresponding to the 
$N$ solutions $\psi^L_i$ above.
Then we use the usual connection on conformal blocks to continue this
insertion to $z = - \infty$ and fuse it to the irregular module again,
choosing again one of $N$ possible channels.
In this way we get operators $S^\pm_{ij}$
on the space of conformal blocks, giving the quantum group action.

This construction should work much more generally: if we have 
a Riemann surface with 3 such irregular insertions we should get 
3 actions of the quantum group, etc.



\subsection{Modular structure}

So far we have discussed a lot of structure on the category $\cC$ 
that comes from studying conformal blocks on the sphere.
We actually get a bit more by considering the torus.

As we already discussed,
by plumbing a sphere to itself we get
a conformal block $\Phi_i$ on the torus $C_q = \bbC^\times / (z \sim qz)$ for each simple module $\cS_i$,
and the $\Phi_i$ span the space $V = \Conf(C_q)$.

Now, recall that the mapping class group of the torus acts on $V$.
(We remarked in \fixme{...} that the action of the mapping class
group is in general only projective; but the case of the torus is an
exception, because we have a global section of the bundle of complex
projective structures.) So we get a representation of $\SL(2,\bbZ)$ on $V$.
In particular the standard $S$ generator acts; let $S_{ij}$ denote its
matrix elements, i.e.
\begin{equation}
  S(\Phi_i) = \sum_j S_{ij} \Phi_j
\end{equation}
Note that $S^2$ gives the action of $-1 \in \SL(2,\bbZ)$, which is
\begin{equation}
  S^2 \Phi_i = \Phi_{i^*} \, .
\end{equation}
implying
\begin{equation}
  (S^{-1})_{ij} = S_{i^* j} = S_{i j^*} \, .
\end{equation}
The torus $C_q$ created by plumbing comes with distinguished
$A$ and $B$ cycles: the $A$ cycle comes from the unit circle in the
original sphere, the $B$ cycle comes from the positive real line (when 
$q$ is real).
For each of these cycles and each simple module $\cS_i$ we get
a Verlinde loop operator; so we have operators $A_i$, $B_i$ acting
on $V$.
The action of $A_i$ is particularly simple: it acts already
\ti{before} the plumbing, so it must be diagonal in the basis of 
the $\Phi_j$, 
\begin{equation}
  A_i \Phi_j = \lambda_{ij} \Phi_j \, .
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the basis
% \begin{equation}
%   B_i (S \Phi_j) = \lambda_{ij} (S \Phi_k) \, ,
% \end{equation}
$\Psi_j = S \Phi_j$ diagonalizes the $B_i$.
On the other hand, $B_i$ creates a copy of $\cS_j$ and moves it
close to $z = 0$, and moves $\cS_j^*$ close to $z = \infty$,
where they can fuse with the $\cS_i$ and $\cS_i^*$ already there:
in other words $B_i$ acts by fusion with $\Phi_i$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
  B_i \Phi_j = N_{ij}^k \Phi_k \, .
\end{equation}
Thus $S$ diagonalizes the fusion operation. From here, it is a

\subsection{Chern-Simons theory}

Fix a compact, simply connected Lie group $G$ and 
level $k\in \mathbb Z$. Also fix a connection $\de + A$ on a (trivialized)\footnote{Fortunately, when $G$ is compact
simply connected, every principal $G$-bundle over a $3$-manifold is trivializable.}
principal $G$-bundle over a closed oriented 
$3$-manifold $M$. Then the \ti{Chern--Simons action} is
\[
S_{\mathrm{CS}}(A)
=
\frac{k}{4\pi} \int_M \Tr \left(
A\wedge \de A + \frac23 A \wedge A \wedge A
\right).
\]
Then \ti{formally} the \ti{Chern-Simons partition function} is
\[
Z_{\mathrm{CS}}(M)
=
\int \mathcal D A \; e^{\, \I S_{\mathrm{CS}}(A)} \, .
\]
Here the integral is supposed to run over connections \ti{modulo gauge transformations}.
It is important that the action $S_{\mathrm CS}(A)$ is invariant 
under gauge transformations, up to a multiple of $2 \pi$; this is why we
needed $k \in \bbZ$.
Witten \cite{Witten1989Jones} introduced this quantum field theory and
gave a method of computing the partition function, which was more fully developed 
in \cite{ReshetikhinTuraev1991}.

There is a pesky detail we should discuss.
In order to calculate the partition function one needs to introduce 
a Riemannian metric $g$ on $M$.
Formally one would
expect the partition function to be independent of $g$, but 
actually this is not quite right:
there is an \ti{anomaly}, as follows.
If $g_0$ and $g_1$ are two Riemannian metrics on $M$, then
fix a metric $g$ on $\widetilde{M} = M \times[0,1]$, 
restricting to $g_0$ and $g_1$ at the two ends.
Let $R\in \Omega^2(\widetilde{M},\End(T\widetilde{M}))$ be the
curvature of $g$.
Then we have
\[
\frac{Z_{\mathrm{CS}}(M,g_1)}{Z_{\mathrm{CS}}(M,g_0)}
=
\exp \left[ -\frac{2 \pi \I\, c}{24}\int_{M\times[0,1]} p_1(R) \right] \, .
\]
where $p_1$ denotes the first Pontryagin form,
\[
p_1(R) = -\frac{1}{8\pi^2}\Tr(R\wedge R) \, .
\]

\begin{exercise} Prove that $p_1$ is conformally invariant.
\end{exercise}

So $Z_{\CS}$ is ``almost topological:'' it depends on the metric, but 
in a simple and controllable way.

\section{Conventions on Lie algebras} \label{sec:lie-conventions}

For a simple Lie algebra $\fg$,
there is a canonical invariant bilinear form
$\kappa$ on $\fg$, inducing $\kappa^{-1}$ on $\fg^*$. 
We normalize it so that the long roots $\alpha$ of $\fg$
have $\kappa^{-1}(\alpha,\alpha) = 2$.

The quadratic Casimir operator is defined by 
\begin{equation}
  C_2 = \sum_i X^i X^i
\end{equation}
where $(X^i)$ is an orthonormal basis of $\fg$.
In this basis we write 
\begin{equation}
  [X^i, X^j] = f^{ijk} X^k
\end{equation}
and the structure constant $f^{ijk}$ is antisymmetric in all exchanges of indices.

The dual Coxeter number $h^\vee$ is defined by the property that
$C_2$ acts in the adjoint representation by the scalar $2 h^\vee$.
i.e. for any $Y \in \fg$
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^{\dim \fg} [X^i, [X^i, Y]] = 2 h^\vee Y \, .
\end{equation}
Taking $Y = X^j$ and summing repeated indices this is also
\begin{equation}
  [X^i, f^{ijk} X^k] = f^{ik\ell} f^{ijk} X^\ell = 2 h^\vee X^j
\end{equation}
i.e.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:f-coxeter}
  f^{ik\ell} f^{ijk} = 2 h^\vee \delta^{j \ell}
\end{equation}

When $\fg = \fsl(N)$, the normalized form is
\begin{equation}
  \kappa(X,Y) = \Tr (XY)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  h^\vee = N \, .
\end{equation}

The quadratic Casimir operator for $\fg = \fsl(2)$ is
\begin{equation}
  C_2 = \frac12 H^2 + EF + FE \, .
\end{equation}
In the representation with highest weight $n \omega$, it has 
eigenvalue $C_2 = \frac12 n(n+2)$.
In particular, for the fundamental representation we have $C_2 = \frac32$.

For any root $\alpha \in \fh^*$ the corresponding coroot $\alpha^\vee$ is determined by the condition
\begin{equation}
  \alpha^\vee \cdot \lambda = \frac{2 \kappa^{-1}(\lambda,\alpha)}{\kappa^{-1}(\alpha,\alpha)}
\end{equation}
i.e.
\begin{equation}
  \alpha^\vee = \frac{2 \alpha^\sharp}{\kappa^{-1}(\alpha,\alpha)}
\end{equation}

We use $\theta \in \fh^*$ to denote the \ti{highest root}: it is the one maximal in the partial order
(i.e. $\theta + \alpha_i$ is not a root for any simple root $\alpha_i$.)
For $\fg = \fsl(N)$, $\theta = \alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_{N-1}$, with root space spanned by $E_{1N}$.
The corresponding coroot is $\theta^\vee = E_{11} - E_{NN} = [E_{1N}, E_{N1}]$.

A \ti{Cartan involution} is a Lie algebra involution $\theta: \fg \to \fg$.
If $\fg$ is complex and 
$\theta$ commutes with the compact involution $\tau$, then
we can define a real form of $\fg$ as the fixed locus of $\sigma = \theta \circ \tau$.
For example, for $\fg = \fsl(N,\bbC)$ we can take $\theta(X) = - X^T$,
$\tau(X) = - X^*$, so $\sigma(X) = \overline{X}$,
and the corresponding real form is $\fsl(N,\bbR)$.
We can also take $\theta(X) = X$,
so $\sigma(X) = - X^*$,
and the corresponding real form is $\fsu(N)$.

\section{The zeta-regularized determinant} \label{sec:zeta-determinant}
 
In the theory of the free boson one expects that
\begin{equation}
  \IP{1} = \sqrt{A} / \sqrt{\det{}' \Delta}
\end{equation}
Since $\Delta$ acts on an infinite-dimensional space we need to explain what we mean by
its determinant.
Precisely we mean the \ti{zeta-regularized} determinant.

Define 
\begin{equation}
 \zeta_\Delta(s) = \sum_{k \ge1 }\lambda_k^{-s} 
\end{equation}
for
$\re(s) > 1$, where $0=\lambda_0<\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\cdots$ are the eigenvalues of $\Delta$.
In fact this function admits meromorphic continuation to the full
$s$-plane. To see this consider the operator $\e^{-t \Delta}$ 
giving heat flow for time $t$. This is a nice
integral operator with a smooth kernel $K_t(x,y)$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
  \e^{-t \Delta} f = \int_{y \in C} K_t(x,y) f(y) \, .
\end{equation}
As $t \to 0$ the kernel $K_t$ 
concentrates around the diagonal (it converges in some distributional sense to a $\delta$-function). On the diagonal we have\footnote{To understand the leading term $\frac{1}{4 \pi t}$, note the heat kernel on $\bbR^2$ is a Gaussian, $K_t(x,y) = \frac{1}{4 \pi t} \exp\left(- \frac{\abs{x-y}^2}{4t} \right)$.}
\begin{equation}
  K_t(x,x) \sim \frac{1}{4 \pi t} \left(1 + \frac{R}{6} t + \cdots\right)
\end{equation}
We can subtract these leading divergent terms from $K_t$,
to get a new kernel $\widetilde K_t(x) = K_t(x,x) - \frac{1}{4 \pi t} - \frac{R}{24 \pi}$.
Now, for $\re(s) > 1$ we have an integral representation
\begin{equation}
  \zeta_\Delta(s) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(s)} \int_0^\infty \frac{\de t}{t} t^s \Tr{}' \e^{-t \Delta}
\end{equation}

With the analytic continuation computed, we finally have
\begin{equation}
\det{}'\Delta = \exp\big(-\zeta'_{\Delta}(0)\big)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Link invariants}

In fact you get a little more, if $\vertex$ is sufficiently nice, e.g. \ti{strongly rational}. 

To get an example of a strongly rational vertex algebra
consider $V^k(\fg)$; it is simple for generic $k$, meaning
that as a module over itself it contains no proper submodule; 
for $k \in \bbZ_+$ it is not simple\footnote{A simple example: in $V^k(\fsl_2)$ the element $E_{-1}^{k+1} \vac$ generates a proper
submodule.} 
but it has a simple quotient $L_k(\fg)$, and $L_k(\fg)$ is strongly rational. Similarly $\Vir^c$ has strongly rational quotients
for special values of $c$.

If $\vertex$ is strongly rational, then it has only finitely many irreducible representations.\footnote{Fine print: I really mean \ti{ordinary modules}.}
Moreover the spaces $\Conf(C, \vertex)$ are finite-dimensional, 
for any compact $C$, with any irreducible representations inserted at punctures. So this is a nice world where all vector spaces
around are finite-dimensional, and we do not have to worry
about functional analysis, double duals, ind- or pro-objects, etc.

Now suppose $C$ is $\bbC\bbP^1$ with $n$ 
marked points $z_1, \dots, z_n$ in the finite plane, each one carrying a representation of $\vertex$. 
Then consider
moving any marked point $z_i$ along any loop in $C \setminus \{z_1, \dots, \hat{z}_i, \dots, z_n \}$.
The parallel transport of the connection $\nabla$ along 
this loop then gives an automorphism of $\Conf(C,\vertex)$.
Thus $\Conf(C,\vertex)$ carries an action of the braid group on $n$ strands.

Moreover you have, in addition to the braiding, ``cup'' and ``cap''
operations which create/destroy a pair of marked points.
Combining these you can build 
framed link invariants: indeed, letting $C$ be
$\bbC\bbP^1$ with \ti{no} marked points, each link in $\bbR^3$
gives a linear operator acting on a finite-dimensional space $\Conf(C, \vertex)$, 
and its trace is a link invariant.

One basic example: if $\vertex = L_k(\fsl_2)$ with $k \in \bbZ_+$, then the link invariant we get
is the \ti{Jones polynomial} $J_L(q)$, with $q = \exp(2 \pi \I / (k+2))$.

In this case
the representations $\vrep$ are objects of a \ti{modular tensor category}.


\subsection{Holomorphic factorization} 

In a 2-dimensional conformal field theory
one computes correlation functions of observables. 
These correlation functions are almost factorized into products of ``holomorphic'' and ``antiholomorphic'' parts,
but not exactly factorized; the nontrivial space of conformal blocks is the obstruction to factorization.

For example, in the CFT of the \ti{free boson} $\Phi$,
one is interested in correlation functions of the form
\begin{equation}
  \DIP{J(z_1) \cdots J(z_n) \bJ(w_1) \cdots \bJ(w_m)} 
\end{equation}
where classically $J = \partial \Phi$ and $\bJ = \overline\partial \Phi$.
Then one finds an approximate factorization
\begin{equation}
  \DIP{J(z_1) \cdots J(z_n) \bJ(w_1) \cdots \bJ(w_m)} = \int \de a \, \IP{J(z_1) \cdots J(z_n)}_{\Psi_a} \IP{\bJ(w_1) \cdots \bJ(w_m)}_{\overline\Psi_a}
\end{equation}
where $\IP{J(z_1) \cdots J(z_n)}_{\Psi_a}$ are holomorphic in a strong sense --- they
are holomorphic in the $z_i$, in $a$, and in the moduli of $C$.
They arise as part of the data of a conformal block $\Psi_a \in \Conf(C, \Heis)$.
So the conformal blocks $\Psi_a$ are the basic building-blocks one uses to build the 
correlation functions of the CFT.

has conformal blocks $\Psi_a$ parameterized by $a \in \bbC$, with the correlation function
\begin{equation}
  \IP{1}_{\Psi_a} = \frac{1}{\eta(\tau)} \exp\left(\frac{1}{4 \pi \I} a^2 \tau\right)
\end{equation}
The blocks relevant for the free boson are the ones with $a \in \I \bbR$.
Then we have
\begin{equation} \label{eq:factorization-free-boson}
  \DIP{1} = \int_{a \in \I \bbR} \left(  \frac{\de a}{2 \pi \I} \right) \, \abs{\IP{1}_{\Psi_a}}^2  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \im \tau} \abs{\eta(\tau)}^2}
\end{equation}
There is a similar, but more elaborate, story for 
higher genus surfaces \cite{McIntyreTakhtajan2006}.

\section{The free scalar field CFT}

\subsection{The action and its conformal invariance}

Let $C$ be a Riemannian 2-manifold, with metric $g$.

We begin with a basic example of a Euclidean, 2-dimensional conformal field theory:
the (uncompactified) \ti{free scalar field}.
It has been made rigorous under the name
\ti{Gaussian free field}.
See e.g. \cite{GuillarmouRhodesVargas2019Polyakov2d}.
But we will stick to the more informal treatment.

We consider the (loosely understood) space of fields on $C$, 
\begin{equation}
  \cF = \left\{ \Phi: C \to \bbR \, \Bigg\lvert  \int_C \Phi \, \de \vol = 0 \right\} \, .
\end{equation}
We are interested in studying ``expectation values'' of random observables on $\cF$, formally
\begin{equation}
  \IP{\cO} = \int_{\Phi \in \cF} \de \mu(\Phi) \, \cO(\Phi) \, \e^{-S(\Phi)}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is a measure on $\cF$,
 $\cO$ is some function on $\cF$, and the \ti{action} $S: \cF \to \bbR$ is\footnote{We always use the Einstein summation convention of summing over repeated indices; cotangent indices are written as 
 subscripts and tangent indices are written as superscripts; thus $g_{ij}$ is the metric on $C$ in components i.e. $g_{ij} = g(\partial_{x^i}, \partial_{x^j})$, 
 and $g^{ij}$ is the inverse metric in components.}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:free-boson-action}
  S(\Phi) = \frac{1}{8\pi} \int_C \norm{\de \Phi}^2 \, \de \vol 
  % = \frac{1}{8 \pi} \int_C \de \Phi \wedge \star \de \Phi 
  % = -\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_C \Phi \, \de \star \de \Phi 
  = \frac{1}{8\pi}\int_C \partial_i \Phi \, \partial_j \Phi \, g^{ij} \sqrt{\det g} \, \de^2 x
  = \frac{1}{8 \pi} \int_C \Phi \Delta \Phi \, \de \vol \, .
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta$ denotes the positive-definite Laplace operator on $C$, acting on functions,
given by
\begin{equation}
  \Delta = \de^* \de = - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}} \partial_i \left(\sqrt{\det g}\,g^{ij}\partial_j\right).
\end{equation}

An important feature here is that the action 
is \ti{conformally invariant}:
for any function $\omega: C \to \bbR$, 
if we rescale the metric on $C$ by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:conformal-rescaling}
  g \to \e^{2 \omega} g \, ,
\end{equation}
the action $S(\Phi)$ for any given $\Phi$ is unchanged.

\begin{exercise}
Verify the conformal invariance of \eqref{eq:free-boson-action}. Would it still be true in dimensions other than $2$?
\end{exercise}

It is useful to study the critical points of $S$.
Making a small variation $\Phi \to \Phi + \delta \Phi$ 
we get
\begin{equation}
  \delta S = \frac{1}{8 \pi} \int_C (\delta \Phi \Delta \Phi + \Phi \Delta (\delta \Phi)) \de \vol = \frac{1}{4 \pi} \int_C \delta \Phi \Delta \Phi \, \de \vol
\end{equation}
This procedure defines the \ti{variational derivative}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\delta S}{\delta \Phi(x)} = \frac{1}{4 \pi} \Delta \Phi(x) \, .
\end{equation}
Requiring it to vanish
gives the \ti{equation of motion}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:scalar-equation-of-motion}
  \Delta \Phi = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
It is equivalent to requiring that
$\delta S$ vanishes for all (say, smooth) variations
$\delta \Phi$.


\subsection{Complex structure}

A \ti{conformal structure} on $C$ means a Riemannian metric $g$
up to local rescalings \eqref{eq:conformal-rescaling}. 
Now recall that
a conformal structure and orientation on $C$ 
together are equivalent to a complex structure,
making $C$ into a Riemann surface.
The operator of multiplication by $\I$ in $T^* C$ 
is induced by a rotation by a quarter-turn clockwise, which is
also equivalent to the Hodge $\star$ operator
acting on 1-forms.\footnote{Relative 
to an oriented orthonormal basis of $T^* C$ we have
$\star(1) = e_1 \wedge e_2$, $\star(e_1) = e_2$, $\star(e_2) = -e_1$, $\star (e_1 \wedge e_2) = 1$.}

Thus the conformal invariance
of \eqref{eq:free-boson-action} is equivalent to saying that it depends only on 
the complex structure, not on the whole Riemannian metric.
We can rewrite it to make this more manifest:\footnote{$(\Delta f) \de \vol = -2 \I \partial \bar\partial f$.}
\begin{equation}
S = \frac{\I}{4 \pi} \int_C \partial \Phi \wedge \bar\partial \Phi \, .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Holomorphic factorization}
The equation of motion \eqref{eq:scalar-equation-of-motion} says, at least locally, that $\Phi$ is the
real part of a holomorphic function: 
\begin{equation}
  \Phi = \phi + \bphi, \qquad \bar\partial \phi = 0 \, .
\end{equation}

This is a hint that the theory will have some kind of factorization
into holomorphic and antiholomorphic parts.
However, note that $\phi$ is not uniquely determined
by $\Phi$: we have the freedom to shift $\phi$ by an imaginary
constant.
This suggests that the factorization will not be perfect.

\subsection{Currents}

We have two conserved currents: if we write
\begin{equation}
  j = \de \Phi, \qquad \tj = \star \de \Phi
\end{equation}
then using \eqref{eq:scalar-equation-of-motion} we have
\begin{equation}
  \de j = 0, \qquad \de \tj = 0 \, .
\end{equation}

Equivalently, we can 
decompose
\begin{equation}
  j = J + \bJ
\end{equation}
according to type, and the two pieces are separately closed: 
\begin{equation}
\bar\partial J = 0, \qquad \partial \bJ = 0 \, .
\end{equation}


\subsection{The energy-momentum tensor}

We define
\begin{equation}
  T_{ij}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\det g}} \frac{\delta S}{\delta g^{ij}(x)} \, .
\end{equation}
This is the classical energy-momentum tensor. 
A direct computation gives
\begin{equation} \label{eq:classical-T-free-boson}
 T_{ij} = \frac{1}{4\pi} \left( \partial_i \Phi \partial_j \Phi - \frac12 g_{ij} \partial_k \Phi \partial^k \Phi \right)
\end{equation}

\subsection{TFT and factorization}

Let's consider now a 3-dimensional field theory.
Let $M$ be a compact oriented 3-manifold, now without a
Riemannian metric.
Instead of a scalar $\Phi$ we have a $1$-form $a$, and we write the action
\begin{equation}
S(a) = \int_M a \wedge \de a \, .  
\end{equation}
(We could call this ``Chern-Simons theory with gauge group $\bbR$.'')
$S$ is invariant under $a \to a + \de \chi$ where $\chi$
is any function: indeed the variation is
\begin{equation}
  \delta S = \int_M \de \chi \wedge \de a = \int_M \de(\chi \wedge \de a) = \int_{\partial M} \chi \wedge \de a
\end{equation}
which vanishes for $M$ without boundary.
The equations of motion require $\de a = 0$.
Thus the space of classical solutions modulo gauge symmetry
is $H^1(M,\bbR)$.

Then we let $M = C \times I$, for $I$ an interval.
To formulate the field theory on $M$ we need (elliptic) 
boundary conditions at the two ends of $I$.
One possibility which works is to add an additional
coupling on the boundary, of the form
\begin{equation}
  \pm \int_{\partial M} \norm{a}^2
\end{equation}
Note that this coupling uses a conformal structure on $C$.



Now one of the principles of Lagrangian field theory is that an equation which holds classically
also holds in correlation functions. (You can prove it formally by integration by parts.) 
So in particular we expect $\bar\partial J = 0$, $\partial \bJ = 0$ to hold in correlation 
functions. Thus, for instance, we can compute correlation functions
\begin{equation}
  \DIP{J(z_1) \cdots J(z_k) \bJ(w_1) \cdots \bJ(w_l)}
\end{equation}
and they should be holomorphic 1-forms in all of the variables $z_i$,
antiholomorphic 1-forms in all of the variables $w_i$.
More precisely they are holomorphic \ti{away from} the diagonals where some $z_i = z_j$
or $w_i = w_j$. 

A second key property of these correlation functions is the \ti{operator product expansion}:
this says (in correlation functions) that around $z_1 = z_2$ we have
\begin{equation}
  J(z_1) J(z_2) = \frac{1}{(z_1 - z_2)^2} + \regular
\end{equation}
